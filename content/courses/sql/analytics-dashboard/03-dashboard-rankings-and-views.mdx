## Leaderboards: three ways to rank

"Best sellers per category" needs a ranking **inside** each category: a window function with `PARTITION BY category`. The three ranking functions differ only in how they treat **ties**. Here they are side by side, ranking products by units sold:

<CodeExample output={` category |        product         | units | row_number | rank | dense_rank
----------+------------------------+-------+------------+------+------------
 books    | Algorithms in Pictures |    17 |          1 |    1 |          1
 books    | Clean Python           |     9 |          2 |    2 |          2
 books    | The Bamboo Code        |     9 |          3 |    2 |          2
 kitchen  | Bamboo Chopsticks      |    15 |          1 |    1 |          1
 kitchen  | Rice Cooker            |     9 |          2 |    2 |          2
 kitchen  | Tea Pot                |     9 |          3 |    2 |          2
(6 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH product_sales AS (
  SELECT p.category, p.name AS product,
    sum(oi.quantity) AS units
  FROM order_items AS oi
  JOIN orders AS o ON o.id = oi.order_id
  JOIN products AS p ON p.id = oi.product_id
  WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
  GROUP BY p.id
)
SELECT category, product, units,
  row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY category
    ORDER BY units DESC, product) AS row_number,
  rank() OVER w AS rank,
  dense_rank() OVER w AS dense_rank
FROM product_sales
WHERE category IN ('books', 'kitchen')
WINDOW w AS (PARTITION BY category ORDER BY units DESC)
ORDER BY category, units DESC, product;
```

</CodeExample>

Clean Python and The Bamboo Code both sold 9 copies:

- **`row_number()`** always numbers rows 1, 2, 3, ties or not. Here it has its own tie-breaker (`ORDER BY units DESC, product`), so Clean Python gets 2 and The Bamboo Code 3. Without a tie-breaker, which of the two gets 2 is arbitrary and can change between runs, and a "top 2" built on it silently drops one of two equal products.
- **`rank()`** gives both 2, and the next product would get 4: it **skips**, like a sports table.
- **`dense_rank()`** gives both 2, and the next product would get 3: no gaps.

For a "top N" tile, `rank()` or `dense_rank()` is usually right: a tie for second place should show both products. Use `row_number()` when you need exactly N rows, and then add a tie-breaker (`ORDER BY units DESC, product`) so the result is at least stable.

## Top 2 per category, with their share

A window function can't go in `WHERE`, because `WHERE` runs **before** windows are computed. So the classic "top N per group" query ranks in a CTE and filters outside. While we're at it, `sum(…) OVER (PARTITION BY category)` gives each category's total on every row, which turns revenue into a **share of the category**:

<CodeExample output={` category |        product         | revenue | category_rank | share_pct
----------+------------------------+---------+---------------+-----------
 books    | Algorithms in Pictures |  493.00 |             1 |        59
 books    | Clean Python           |  224.91 |             2 |        27
 kitchen  | Rice Cooker            |  809.10 |             1 |        70
 kitchen  | Tea Pot                |  288.00 |             2 |        25
 snacks   | Panda Cookies          |   60.00 |             1 |        69
 snacks   | Bamboo Crisps          |   26.91 |             2 |        31
 tea      | Earl Grey              |   42.00 |             1 |        52
 tea      | Jasmine Tea            |   39.00 |             2 |        48
 toys     | Wooden Puzzle          |  182.40 |             1 |        63
 toys     | Kite                   |  105.00 |             2 |        37
(10 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH product_sales AS (
  SELECT p.category, p.name AS product,
    sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS revenue
  FROM order_items AS oi
  JOIN orders AS o ON o.id = oi.order_id
  JOIN products AS p ON p.id = oi.product_id
  WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
  GROUP BY p.id
),
ranked AS (
  SELECT category, product, revenue,
    dense_rank() OVER (PARTITION BY category
      ORDER BY revenue DESC) AS category_rank,
    round(100 * revenue
      / sum(revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY category)) AS share_pct
  FROM product_sales
)
SELECT *
FROM ranked
WHERE category_rank <= 2
ORDER BY category, category_rank, product;
```

</CodeExample>

The Rice Cooker alone makes 70% of kitchen revenue. That's a risk worth a tile of its own: if its supplier has a problem, the whole category collapses.

## Which customers matter most?

Another favourite tile: "our top customers bring X% of revenue". A **running total** of revenue, sorted from the biggest customer down, divided by the grand total `sum(…) OVER ()`, gives the cumulative share:

<CodeExample output={`    name     | revenue | cumulative_pct | quartile
-------------+---------+----------------+----------
 Ravi Patel  |  282.23 |           11.5 |        1
 Sam Ortiz   |  273.83 |           22.7 |        1
 Sofia Rossi |  260.13 |           33.4 |        1
 Diego López |  258.17 |           43.9 |        1
 Kenji Mori  |  207.67 |           52.4 |        1
 Mei Lin     |  200.17 |           60.6 |        2
(6 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH customer_revenue AS (
  SELECT c.name, sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS revenue
  FROM customers AS c
  JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.id
  JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
  WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
  GROUP BY c.id
)
SELECT name, revenue,
  round(100 * sum(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY revenue DESC, name)
    / sum(revenue) OVER (), 1) AS cumulative_pct,
  ntile(4) OVER (ORDER BY revenue DESC, name) AS quartile
FROM customer_revenue
ORDER BY revenue DESC, name
LIMIT 6;
```

</CodeExample>

The top 5 of 17 customers bring in over half the revenue. Businesses call this the **Pareto** pattern ("80/20"), and it decides where the effort goes: those five deserve a personal thank-you email. **`ntile(4)`** splits the customers into four nearly equal groups (quartiles), handy for "top 25% customers" segments.

Notice the tie-breaker `name` in **both** window `ORDER BY`s. Without it, two customers with equal revenue could be summed in either order, and the running total would differ between runs.

## Trend lines: a 4-week moving average

Weekly revenue jumps around: one big Rice Cooker order makes a week look amazing. A **moving average** smooths the line by averaging each week with the three before it. It needs two things you've seen already: a **gap-free calendar** (a missing week would make "the three weeks before" wrong) and a **window frame**:

<CodeExample output={`    week    | revenue | avg_4_weeks
------------+---------+-------------
 2025-04-07 |   52.20 |       98.11
 2025-04-14 |  114.74 |      124.69
 2025-04-21 |   22.80 |       92.96
 2025-04-28 |   17.85 |       51.90
 2025-05-05 |  109.50 |       66.22
 2025-05-12 |  200.17 |       87.58
 2025-05-19 |    8.40 |       83.98
 2025-05-26 |  207.73 |      131.45
 2025-06-02 |  124.10 |      135.10
 2025-06-09 |   52.20 |       98.11
 2025-06-16 |   36.24 |      105.07
(11 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH paid_orders AS (
  SELECT o.id, o.ordered_at,
    sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS total
  FROM orders AS o
  JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
  WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
  GROUP BY o.id
),
weeks AS (
  SELECT generate_series(date '2025-01-06', date '2025-06-16',
    interval '1 week')::date AS week
),
weekly AS (
  SELECT w.week, coalesce(sum(p.total), 0) AS revenue
  FROM weeks AS w
  LEFT JOIN paid_orders AS p
    ON p.ordered_at >= w.week AND p.ordered_at < w.week + 7
  GROUP BY w.week
),
smoothed AS (
  SELECT week, revenue,
    CASE WHEN count(*) OVER last_4 = 4
      THEN round(avg(revenue) OVER last_4, 2)
    END AS avg_4_weeks
  FROM weekly
  WINDOW last_4 AS (ORDER BY week
    ROWS BETWEEN 3 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)
)
SELECT *
FROM smoothed
WHERE week >= '2025-04-07'
ORDER BY week;
```

</CodeExample>

- **`ROWS BETWEEN 3 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW`** is the frame: this week and the three rows before it. `ROWS` counts rows, which is why the calendar must have one row per week with no gaps.
- The first three weeks of the year don't have three weeks before them, so their average would be built from fewer weeks. The `CASE` with **`count(*) OVER last_4 = 4`** shows NULL instead of a misleading partial average.
- The **date filter is in the outer query**, after the window has done its work. Putting `WHERE week >= '2025-04-07'` inside `weekly` would make April's first average NULL, because the March weeks it needs would be gone.

Weekly revenue swings from 8.40 to 207.73, while the average stays between about 50 and 135, so the trend is much easier to read.

## Packaging the dashboard as views

The dashboard tool (Metabase, Grafana, Superset, Looker…) shouldn't contain 40-line queries: nobody reviews them there, and the same "paid order" logic would be copied into every tile. Instead, each building block becomes a **view** in the database, and the tiles query the views:

<CodeExample output={`CREATE VIEW

CREATE VIEW

   month    | orders | active_customers | revenue
------------+--------+------------------+---------
 2025-04-01 |      6 |                6 |  331.69
 2025-05-01 |      5 |                5 |  467.80
 2025-06-01 |      4 |                4 |  270.54
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
CREATE VIEW paid_orders AS
SELECT o.id, o.customer_id, o.ordered_at,
  sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS total
FROM orders AS o
JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
GROUP BY o.id;

CREATE VIEW monthly_kpis AS
SELECT date_trunc('month', ordered_at)::date AS month,
  count(*) AS orders,
  count(DISTINCT customer_id) AS active_customers,
  sum(total) AS revenue
FROM paid_orders
GROUP BY 1;

SELECT *
FROM monthly_kpis
WHERE month >= '2025-04-01'
ORDER BY month;
```

</CodeExample>

Views stack: `monthly_kpis` is built on `paid_orders`, and the definition of a paid order now lives in **exactly one place**. When finance decides that `paid` orders shouldn't count until they ship, you change one view and every tile follows.

A view runs its query **every time** it's read. That's perfect for small, fast queries. For heavy ones (the cohort table over millions of orders, say) use a **materialized view**: it stores the result like a table, and you refresh it on a schedule. The price is that it can be **stale**:

<CodeExample output={`CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW

INSERT 0 1

INSERT 0 1

   month    | revenue
------------+---------
 2025-06-01 |  270.54
(1 row)

REFRESH

   month    | revenue
------------+---------
 2025-06-01 |  360.44
(1 row)`}>

```sql
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW monthly_revenue AS
SELECT date_trunc('month', o.ordered_at)::date AS month,
  sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS revenue
FROM orders AS o
JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
GROUP BY 1;

-- A new order arrives...
INSERT INTO orders (customer_id, ordered_at, status)
VALUES (3, '2025-06-20 12:00', 'paid');
INSERT INTO order_items (order_id, product_id, quantity, unit_price)
SELECT max(id), 15, 1, 89.90 FROM orders;

SELECT * FROM monthly_revenue WHERE month = '2025-06-01';

REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW monthly_revenue;

SELECT * FROM monthly_revenue WHERE month = '2025-06-01';
```

</CodeExample>

The first `SELECT` still shows the old June revenue; only after `REFRESH` does the new Rice Cooker appear. Dashboards built on materialized views say so on screen: "Data as of 06:00 today".

<Callout type="tip" title="Making the dashboard fast in production">

- **Index the time column**: almost every tile filters on `ordered_at` ranges, so an index on `orders (ordered_at)` turns a full scan into a range scan. Keep the filters sargable (`ordered_at >= …`, never `date_trunc(…, ordered_at) = …`).
- **Refresh without blocking readers**: plain `REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW` locks out readers while it runs. `REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW CONCURRENTLY` doesn't, but it needs a **unique index** on the materialized view (here on `month`).
- **Pre-aggregate**: at millions of events a day, teams keep a `daily_stats` table that a nightly job fills with one row per day, and the dashboard reads only that.
- **Use a replica**: heavy analytics queries run on a read replica, so they can't slow down customers' checkouts on the primary.

</Callout>

## The final dashboard query set

Here's the whole dashboard, tile by tile, with the technique behind each one:

| Tile | Query | Built with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue, orders, active customers this month (vs last month) | `monthly_kpis` + `lag()` | `date_trunc`, `count(DISTINCT)`, `nullif` |
| Daily active customers chart | calendar + LEFT JOIN | `generate_series`, conditions in `ON` |
| Active in the last 30 days | range join | `count(DISTINCT)` per calendar day |
| Cohort retention triangle | grid + pivot | `age()`, `max(…) FILTER`, NULL for the future |
| Conversion funnel (by device) | steps `VALUES` + LEFT JOIN | `first_value`, `lag`, `PARTITION BY` |
| Best sellers per category | rank in a CTE, filter outside | `dense_rank`, `sum() OVER (PARTITION BY)` |
| Top customers | running share | `sum() OVER (ORDER BY)`, `ntile` |
| Weekly revenue trend | calendar + moving average | `ROWS BETWEEN 3 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW` |

Every row of that table is something you've now written yourself. 🐼

Time to ship the dashboard. 🚀 The starter already has the `paid_orders` view. Add two more views on top of it, then the query for the best-sellers tile:

1. **`product_leaderboard`** with the columns `category`, `product` (the product's name), `revenue` (from paid orders) and `category_rank`: the `dense_rank()` of the product's revenue within its category, highest first. Only products that sold something appear.
2. **`weekly_revenue`** with the columns `week` (a date, the Monday of each week from `2025-01-06` to `2025-06-16`), `revenue` (`0` for a week without paid orders) and `avg_4_weeks`: the average revenue of that week and the 3 before it, rounded to 2 decimals, or NULL when there aren't 4 weeks yet.
3. Finally, the **best-sellers tile**: select everything from `product_leaderboard` where `category_rank` is at most 2, sorted by category, rank and product.

The checks change the data afterwards and read your views again, so they must be views, not tables.

<Hint>

`product_leaderboard`: join `paid_orders` to `order_items` and `products`, group by `p.id` in a CTE, then add `dense_rank() OVER (PARTITION BY category ORDER BY revenue DESC)`. `weekly_revenue`: copy the moving-average query from this lesson: a `weeks` calendar with `generate_series(date '2025-01-06', date '2025-06-16', interval '1 week')::date`, a LEFT JOIN on the half-open week range with `coalesce(sum(p.total), 0)`, then `CASE WHEN count(*) OVER last_4 = 4 THEN round(avg(revenue) OVER last_4, 2) END` with the `ROWS BETWEEN 3 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW` frame. A view can start with `WITH`.

</Hint>

```sql
CREATE VIEW paid_orders AS
SELECT o.id, o.customer_id, o.ordered_at,
  sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS total
FROM orders AS o
JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
GROUP BY o.id;

CREATE VIEW product_leaderboard AS
WITH product_sales AS (
  SELECT p.category, p.name AS product,
    sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS revenue
  FROM paid_orders AS po
  JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = po.id
  JOIN products AS p ON p.id = oi.product_id
  GROUP BY p.id
)
SELECT category, product, revenue,
  dense_rank() OVER (PARTITION BY category
    ORDER BY revenue DESC) AS category_rank
FROM product_sales;

CREATE VIEW weekly_revenue AS
WITH weeks AS (
  SELECT generate_series(date '2025-01-06', date '2025-06-16',
    interval '1 week')::date AS week
),
weekly AS (
  SELECT w.week, coalesce(sum(p.total), 0) AS revenue
  FROM weeks AS w
  LEFT JOIN paid_orders AS p
    ON p.ordered_at >= w.week AND p.ordered_at < w.week + 7
  GROUP BY w.week
)
SELECT week, revenue,
  CASE WHEN count(*) OVER last_4 = 4
    THEN round(avg(revenue) OVER last_4, 2)
  END AS avg_4_weeks
FROM weekly
WINDOW last_4 AS (ORDER BY week
  ROWS BETWEEN 3 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW);

SELECT *
FROM product_leaderboard
WHERE category_rank <= 2
ORDER BY category, category_rank, product;
```

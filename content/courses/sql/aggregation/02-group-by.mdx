In the last lesson, every aggregate query squeezed the whole table into **one** row. Useful, but real questions are usually "per something": customers **per country**, revenue **per product**, orders **per month**. 📊

**`GROUP BY`** does exactly that. It sorts the rows into **groups** that share a value, then runs your aggregates once for **each group**. One group, one result row.

## One row per group

How many products are in each category, and how much stock do they have?

<CodeExample output={` category | products | units | average_price
----------+----------+-------+---------------
 books    |        3 |    10 |         22.16
 kitchen  |        3 |   210 |         42.03
 snacks   |        3 |   265 |          3.08
 tea      |        3 |    52 |          8.58
 toys     |        3 |    48 |         14.80
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  category,
  count(*) AS products,
  sum(stock) AS units,
  round(avg(price), 2) AS average_price
FROM products
GROUP BY category
ORDER BY category;
```

</CodeExample>

Here's what PostgreSQL does behind the scenes:

```text
1. Split: put the rows into piles by category

   books:   The Bamboo Code, Algorithms in Pictures, Clean Python
   kitchen: Tea Pot, Bamboo Chopsticks, Rice Cooker
   snacks:  Bamboo Crisps, Panda Cookies, Rice Crackers
   ...

2. Aggregate: run count(*), sum(stock), avg(price) on each pile

3. Combine: one result row per pile
```

`GROUP BY` goes after `WHERE` and before `ORDER BY`. Without `ORDER BY`, the groups can come out in any order, so always add one when the order matters.

## The golden rule of GROUP BY

Once a query has `GROUP BY`, every column in the `SELECT` list must be either:

1. **in the `GROUP BY`** (it's the same for the whole group, like `category` above), or
2. **inside an aggregate function** (it's squeezed into one value, like `sum(stock)`).

Anything else is ambiguous. Each category has three product names: which one should PostgreSQL show?

<CodeExample output={`ERROR:  column "products.name" must appear in the GROUP BY clause or be used in an aggregate function
LINE 1: SELECT category, name, count(*) AS products
                         ^`}>

```sql
SELECT category, name, count(*) AS products
FROM products
GROUP BY category;
```

</CodeExample>

To fix this, decide what you actually want: `min(name)` (one name per group), `count(name)`, or add `name` to the `GROUP BY` (but then every product is its own group, which isn't a summary any more).

## Sorting and limiting groups

You can sort groups by an aggregate, using its alias, and keep only the top few with `LIMIT`. Which countries do most of our customers come from?

<CodeExample output={`  country  | customers
-----------+-----------
 UK        |         4
 Japan     |         2
 Russia    |         2
 Australia |         1
 Brazil    |         1
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT country, count(*) AS customers
FROM customers
GROUP BY country
ORDER BY customers DESC, country
LIMIT 5;
```

</CodeExample>

Japan and Russia both have 2 customers. The second sort key, `country`, breaks the tie, so the result is the same every time.

### NULL gets a group too

What if the column you group by has `NULL`s? All the `NULL`s go together into **one** group:

<CodeExample output={`  city  | customers
--------+-----------
 London |         2
 Moscow |         2
 NULL   |         2
 Berlin |         1
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT city, count(*) AS customers
FROM customers
GROUP BY city
ORDER BY customers DESC, city
LIMIT 4;
```

</CodeExample>

The `NULL` row is "customers without a city". It comes after London and Moscow, which also have 2 customers, because an ascending sort puts `NULL` after real values by default (`NULLS FIRST` would change that).

## WHERE first, then GROUP BY

`WHERE` throws rows away **before** they're grouped, so the aggregates only see the rows that are left. How many orders in each status were placed in the first quarter of 2025?

<CodeExample output={`  status   | orders
-----------+--------
 delivered |      9
 cancelled |      4
 paid      |      4
 shipped   |      4
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT status, count(*) AS orders
FROM orders
WHERE ordered_at < '2025-04-01'
GROUP BY status
ORDER BY orders DESC, status;
```

</CodeExample>

## Grouping by a calculation

You can group by any expression, not just a column. This is where [the date functions](/learn/sql/date-functions) from the last module shine: **`date_trunc('month', ordered_at)`** gives every order in the same month the same value, so grouping by it gives one row **per month**:

<CodeExample output={`   month    | orders
------------+--------
 2025-01-01 |      5
 2025-02-01 |      6
 2025-03-01 |      6
 2025-04-01 |      6
 2025-05-01 |      5
 2025-06-01 |      4
(6 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  date_trunc('month', ordered_at)::date AS month,
  count(*) AS orders
FROM orders
WHERE status <> 'cancelled'
GROUP BY month
ORDER BY month;
```

</CodeExample>

PostgreSQL lets you write `GROUP BY month`, using the alias from the `SELECT` list, instead of repeating the whole `date_trunc(...)` expression. You may also see `GROUP BY 1`, which means "the first column of the `SELECT` list". It's handy for quick queries, but it breaks quietly when someone reorders the columns.

Integer division from the numbers lesson makes a neat grouping key, too: `year / 10 * 10` turns 2014 into 2010 and 2023 into 2020, so you get one row per **decade**:

<CodeExample output={` decade | films | average_minutes
--------+-------+-----------------
   2010 |     6 |             104
   2020 |     6 |             109
(2 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  year / 10 * 10 AS decade,
  count(*) AS films,
  round(avg(minutes)) AS average_minutes
FROM movies
GROUP BY decade
ORDER BY decade;
```

</CodeExample>

## Grouping by several columns

List several columns in `GROUP BY`, and you get one group for each **combination** of values. How many people have each job title in each department, and what do they earn on average?

<CodeExample output={` department_id |    job_title    | people | average_salary
---------------+-----------------+--------+----------------
             1 | Architect       |      1 |         105000
             1 | CTO             |      1 |         130000
             1 | Engineer        |      3 |          72333
             1 | Lead Engineer   |      1 |          98000
             3 | Account Manager |      2 |          53000
             3 | Head of Sales   |      1 |          90000
             3 | Sales Rep       |      1 |          41000
(7 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  department_id,
  job_title,
  count(*) AS people,
  round(avg(salary)) AS average_salary
FROM employees
WHERE department_id IN (1, 3)
GROUP BY department_id, job_title
ORDER BY department_id, job_title;
```

</CodeExample>

Engineering (department 1) has three Engineers, so they're one group of 3. Each combination that exists in the data becomes a row; combinations that don't exist (like a Sales Rep in Engineering) simply don't appear. In Level 1 you'll learn to join the `departments` table to show names instead of numbers.

<Callout type="tip" title="GROUP BY with no aggregates is like DISTINCT">

`SELECT country FROM customers GROUP BY country` gives each country once, exactly like `SELECT DISTINCT country FROM customers`. Use `DISTINCT` when you just want the different values, and `GROUP BY` when you also want to count or add something up per value.

</Callout>

Try it yourself: group the movies by director instead of genre, or by genre **and** decade.

Which products bring in the most money? 🏆 Using the `order_items` table (one row per product in an order, with its `quantity` and `unit_price`), show the **5 products with the highest revenue**:

- **`units`**: the total quantity sold,
- **`revenue`**: the total of `quantity * unit_price` over all its order lines.

Sort by revenue, highest first (if two products ever tie, the smaller `product_id` goes first), and keep only the top 5.

<Hint>

Group the order lines with `GROUP BY product_id`, then use `sum(quantity)` and `sum(quantity * unit_price)`. Sort with `ORDER BY revenue DESC, product_id` and finish with `LIMIT 5`.

```sql
SELECT
  product_id,
  sum(quantity) AS units,
  sum(quantity * unit_price) AS revenue
FROM order_items
GROUP BY product_id
ORDER BY revenue DESC, product_id
LIMIT 5;
```

## Recap

- **`GROUP BY column`** splits rows into groups with the same value and runs the aggregates once per group: one result row per group.
- The golden rule: every selected column is either **in the `GROUP BY`** or **inside an aggregate**.
- Clause order: `SELECT … FROM … WHERE … GROUP BY … ORDER BY … LIMIT`. `WHERE` filters rows **before** grouping.
- Sort groups by an aggregate alias (`ORDER BY customers DESC, country`), with a tie-breaker, and use `LIMIT` for a top-N.
- `NULL`s form one group of their own.
- Group by expressions like `date_trunc('month', ordered_at)` for per-month reports, and by several columns for one row per combination.

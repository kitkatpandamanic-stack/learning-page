In [part 1](/learn/sql/shop-revenue-by-month) you worked out **how much** the shop sells. The board's next questions are about **who** buys and **what** they buy:

> 2. Who are our best customers, and do people come back?
> 3. Which products never sell?

These questions are all about **comparing two tables**: customers with their orders, products with their sales. You'll lean on joins, `LEFT JOIN`, `NOT EXISTS` and CTEs, and you'll meet one of the sneakiest bugs in reporting SQL along the way.

## The best customers

Start from the same building block as part 1, one row per order with its total, then add it up **per customer**:

<CodeExample output={`    name     | country | orders | spent
-------------+---------+--------+--------
 Ravi Patel  | India   |      3 | 282.23
 Sam Ortiz   | Spain   |      2 | 273.83
 Sofia Rossi | Italy   |      3 | 260.13
 Diego López | Mexico  |      2 | 258.17
 Kenji Mori  | Japan   |      2 | 207.67
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH order_totals AS (
  SELECT o.id, o.customer_id, o.status,
    sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS total
  FROM orders AS o
  JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
  GROUP BY o.id
)
SELECT c.name, c.country,
  count(*) AS orders,
  sum(t.total) AS spent
FROM order_totals AS t
JOIN customers AS c ON c.id = t.customer_id
WHERE t.status <> 'cancelled'
GROUP BY c.id
ORDER BY spent DESC, c.name
LIMIT 5;
```

</CodeExample>

Ravi Patel tops the list with three orders. Notice the tie-breaker `c.name` in `ORDER BY`: if two customers spent the same, the list still comes out in the same order every time, which matters when the report is rerun next month.

## Do customers come back?

A **repeat buyer** is a customer with at least two (non-cancelled) orders. Repeat buyers are gold for a shop: winning a new customer costs far more than keeping one. Let's compute a `customer_stats` step and compare the two groups:

<CodeExample output={` repeat_buyer | customers | revenue | avg_spent
--------------+-----------+---------+-----------
 t            |        12 | 1918.25 |    159.85
 f            |         5 |  527.57 |    105.51
(2 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH customer_stats AS (
  SELECT o.customer_id,
    count(DISTINCT o.id) AS orders,
    sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS spent
  FROM orders AS o
  JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
  WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
  GROUP BY o.customer_id
)
SELECT orders >= 2 AS repeat_buyer,
  count(*) AS customers,
  sum(spent) AS revenue,
  round(avg(spent), 2) AS avg_spent
FROM customer_stats
GROUP BY repeat_buyer
ORDER BY repeat_buyer DESC;
```

</CodeExample>

Twelve of the seventeen customers came back, and they bring in almost **80% of revenue**. Two details make this query work:

- `count(DISTINCT o.id)` counts orders even though the join made one row per item (the fan-out trap from part 1).
- `orders >= 2 AS repeat_buyer` is a **boolean expression used as a column**, and you can `GROUP BY` it like any other column: `t` for repeat buyers, `f` for one-time buyers. No special syntax needed to split customers into two groups.

## Customers who never ordered

The customers who signed up but never bought anything don't appear in `orders` at all, so no query that starts from `orders` can find them. You need customers **without** a matching order: an **anti-join**. Both classic forms work:

<CodeExample output={`    name    | joined_on
------------+------------
 Grace Kim  | 2024-11-23
 Tom Baker  | 2024-12-12
 Nina Novak | 2025-01-05
(3 rows)

    name    | joined_on
------------+------------
 Grace Kim  | 2024-11-23
 Tom Baker  | 2024-12-12
 Nina Novak | 2025-01-05
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
-- LEFT JOIN, keep the rows that found no order
SELECT c.name, c.joined_on
FROM customers AS c
LEFT JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.id
WHERE o.id IS NULL
ORDER BY c.joined_on;

-- NOT EXISTS says the same thing in words
SELECT c.name, c.joined_on
FROM customers AS c
WHERE NOT EXISTS (
  SELECT 1 FROM orders AS o WHERE o.customer_id = c.id
)
ORDER BY c.joined_on;
```

</CodeExample>

Three customers joined in the last few months of 2024 and early 2025 and haven't ordered yet: perfect for a welcome discount email. [`NOT EXISTS`](/learn/sql/queries-inside-queries) reads closest to the question ("customers for whom no order exists") and is the safest choice, as you're about to see.

<Callout type="warning" title="Avoid NOT IN with a subquery">

`WHERE c.id NOT IN (SELECT customer_id FROM orders)` looks like a third way, and it works here. But if the subquery ever returns a `NULL`, `NOT IN` returns **no rows at all**, because `x NOT IN (1, NULL)` is unknown rather than true. `NOT EXISTS` doesn't have this trap, so make it your habit.

</Callout>

## Best sellers

On to products. Which ones bring in the most money? Join items to products and orders, leave out cancelled orders, and rank by revenue:

<CodeExample output={`          name          | category | units | revenue
------------------------+----------+-------+---------
 Rice Cooker            | kitchen  |     9 |  809.10
 Algorithms in Pictures | books    |    17 |  493.00
 Tea Pot                | kitchen  |     9 |  288.00
 Clean Python           | books    |     9 |  224.91
 Wooden Puzzle          | toys     |    16 |  182.40
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT p.name, p.category,
  sum(oi.quantity) AS units,
  sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS revenue
FROM order_items AS oi
JOIN orders AS o ON o.id = oi.order_id
JOIN products AS p ON p.id = oi.product_id
WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
GROUP BY p.id
ORDER BY revenue DESC, p.name
LIMIT 5;
```

</CodeExample>

The Rice Cooker is the star: only 9 units, but at 89.90 each. Ranking by `units` instead would put very different products on top, so it's worth showing the board both numbers.

## Products that never sold: a trap

"Which products never sell?" sounds like the customers question again: products with no order items. Let's try:

<CodeExample output={` name
------
(0 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT p.name
FROM products AS p
LEFT JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.product_id = p.id
WHERE oi.product_id IS NULL;
```

</CodeExample>

No rows: every product has been ordered at some point. Case closed? Not quite. **Ordered** isn't **sold**: some orders were cancelled. A product that only ever appeared in cancelled orders never brought in a penny. So the question really is: products with no item **in a non-cancelled order**.

A natural first attempt adds the status filter to the `WHERE` clause, and it goes badly wrong:

<CodeExample output={` name
------
(0 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT p.name
FROM products AS p
LEFT JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.product_id = p.id
LEFT JOIN orders AS o ON o.id = oi.order_id
WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
  AND oi.product_id IS NULL;
```

</CodeExample>

Still nothing, and this time it's a bug. For a product with no matching row, `o.status` is `NULL`, and `NULL <> 'cancelled'` is not true, so `WHERE` throws away **exactly the rows the anti-join was looking for**. A condition on the right-hand table in `WHERE` quietly turns a `LEFT JOIN` back into an inner join.

The fix is to put the condition **inside** the test for a matching row. With `NOT EXISTS`, that's natural:

<CodeExample output={`     name      | category | stock
---------------+----------+-------
 Matcha Powder | tea      |    12
 Plush Panda   | toys     |    25
 Rice Crackers | snacks   |    60
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT p.name, p.category, p.stock
FROM products AS p
WHERE NOT EXISTS (
  SELECT 1
  FROM order_items AS oi
  JOIN orders AS o ON o.id = oi.order_id
  WHERE oi.product_id = p.id
    AND o.status <> 'cancelled'
)
ORDER BY p.name;
```

</CodeExample>

Here the "match" spans two tables (an item **and** its order), which is awkward to express with `LEFT JOIN`s but reads naturally as a subquery. Three products have never really sold, and together they tie up 97 units of stock: Matcha Powder appeared in eight orders, and every single one was cancelled. That's a story for the board (is the price wrong? is the delivery time too long?), and a naive query would have hidden it completely.

<Callout type="tip" title="Filters on the optional side go in ON">

With a `LEFT JOIN`, a condition on the **optional** table belongs in the `ON` clause (or in a `NOT EXISTS` subquery), never in `WHERE`. In `ON`, it decides which rows count as a match; in `WHERE`, it deletes the unmatched rows afterwards. When an anti-join returns nothing, this is the first thing to check.

</Callout>

  WHERE oi.product_id = p.id
    AND o.status <> 'cancelled'

## Recap

- Per-customer and per-product reports start from a join plus `GROUP BY customer` or `GROUP BY product`; use `count(DISTINCT o.id)` to count orders after joining items.
- A boolean expression like `orders >= 2 AS repeat_buyer` can be grouped on to split rows into two groups.
- An **anti-join** finds rows without a match: `LEFT JOIN ... WHERE right.id IS NULL`, or (clearer and safer) `NOT EXISTS`.
- Avoid `NOT IN (subquery)`: one `NULL` in the subquery makes it return nothing.
- Conditions on the optional side of a `LEFT JOIN` belong in `ON` or inside `NOT EXISTS`; in `WHERE` they silently turn it into an inner join.
- "Ordered" isn't "sold": always ask which statuses a business question should count.

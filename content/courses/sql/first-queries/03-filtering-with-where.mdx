So far every query has returned **all** the rows of a table. Real questions are pickier: "Which products cost less than £5?", "Which customers live in Japan?", "Which films came out after 2020?" The **`WHERE`** clause keeps only the rows that match a condition and leaves the rest out.

## Your first WHERE

Put `WHERE` and a **condition** after `FROM`. The database checks the condition for every row and keeps only the rows where it's **true**:

<CodeExample output={`     name      | price
---------------+-------
 Earl Grey     |  5.25
 Jasmine Tea   |  6.50
 Matcha Powder | 14.00
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, price
FROM products
WHERE category = 'tea'
ORDER BY name;
```

</CodeExample>

- `category = 'tea'` is the condition: "the category is equal to tea".
- `'tea'` is a text value, so it goes in **single quotes**.
- Notice we filtered on `category` without showing it. You can filter on any column of the table.

The parts of a query always come in this order: **`SELECT` → `FROM` → `WHERE` → `ORDER BY`**. Swap them around and you'll get a syntax error.

## Comparing values

These are the **comparison operators**:

| Operator        | Meaning                  | Example             |
| --------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------- |
| `=`             | equal to                 | `country = 'Japan'` |
| `<>` (or `!=`)  | not equal to             | `status <> 'paid'`  |
| `<` and `>`     | less than, greater than  | `price < 5`         |
| `<=` and `>=`   | less/greater or equal to | `year >= 2020`      |

Numbers are written **without** quotes:

<CodeExample output={`       name        | price
-------------------+-------
 Rice Crackers     |  2.50
 Bamboo Crisps     |  2.99
 Panda Cookies     |  3.75
 Bamboo Chopsticks |  4.20
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, price
FROM products
WHERE price < 5
ORDER BY price;
```

</CodeExample>

Dates are written like text, in single quotes, as **year-month-day**. Comparing dates works just like comparing numbers: later dates are "bigger". Here are the customers who joined on or after 1 October 2024:

<CodeExample output={`    name     | joined_on
-------------+------------
 Emma Wilson | 2024-10-16
 Ivan Petrov | 2024-11-04
 Grace Kim   | 2024-11-23
 Tom Baker   | 2024-12-12
 Nina Novak  | 2025-01-05
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, joined_on
FROM customers
WHERE joined_on >= '2024-10-01'
ORDER BY joined_on;
```

</CodeExample>

<Callout type="warning" title="Text comparisons are exact">

`'UK'` and `'uk'` are different values. `WHERE country = 'uk'` finds **nothing**, because every UK customer is stored with capital letters. An empty result isn't an error: you just get `(0 rows)`. If you're surprised by an empty result, check the spelling and the capitals.

</Callout>

## Combining conditions with AND, OR and NOT

Often one condition isn't enough:

- **`AND`**: both conditions must be true.
- **`OR`**: at least one of them must be true.
- **`NOT`**: flips a condition, so true becomes false and false becomes true.

Toys under £15:

<CodeExample output={`     name      | category | price
---------------+----------+-------
 Wooden Puzzle | toys     | 11.40
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, category, price
FROM products
WHERE category = 'toys' AND price < 15
ORDER BY name;
```

</CodeExample>

Everything in the tea **or** kitchen categories:

<CodeExample output={`       name        | category | price
-------------------+----------+-------
 Bamboo Chopsticks | kitchen  |  4.20
 Earl Grey         | tea      |  5.25
 Jasmine Tea       | tea      |  6.50
 Matcha Powder     | tea      | 14.00
 Rice Cooker       | kitchen  | 89.90
 Tea Pot           | kitchen  | 32.00
(6 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, category, price
FROM products
WHERE category = 'tea' OR category = 'kitchen'
ORDER BY name;
```

</CodeExample>

### Use brackets when you mix AND and OR

Say you want **tea or snacks that cost less than £3**. This looks right, but it isn't:

<CodeExample output={`     name      | category | price
---------------+----------+-------
 Bamboo Crisps | snacks   |  2.99
 Earl Grey     | tea      |  5.25
 Jasmine Tea   | tea      |  6.50
 Matcha Powder | tea      | 14.00
 Rice Crackers | snacks   |  2.50
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, category, price
FROM products
WHERE category = 'tea' OR category = 'snacks' AND price < 3
ORDER BY name;
```

</CodeExample>

The expensive teas sneaked in! Just like `×` comes before `+` in maths, **`AND` is worked out before `OR`**. So the database read the condition as "tea, **or** (snacks under £3)". Brackets make it say what you mean:

<CodeExample output={`     name      | category | price
---------------+----------+-------
 Bamboo Crisps | snacks   |  2.99
 Rice Crackers | snacks   |  2.50
(2 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, category, price
FROM products
WHERE (category = 'tea' OR category = 'snacks') AND price < 3
ORDER BY name;
```

</CodeExample>

<Callout type="tip" title="When in doubt, add brackets">

Whenever a condition mixes `AND` and `OR`, wrap the `OR` part in brackets. It costs nothing, and it makes your intention clear to the database and to the next person reading your query.

</Callout>

## IN: matching one of a list

Writing `country = 'Japan' OR country = 'Russia' OR country = 'Italy'` gets long. **`IN`** checks whether a value is one of a list:

<CodeExample output={`     name     | country
--------------+---------
 Anna Ivanova | Russia
 Ivan Petrov  | Russia
 Kenji Mori   | Japan
 Sofia Rossi  | Italy
 Yuki Tanaka  | Japan
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, country
FROM customers
WHERE country IN ('Japan', 'Russia', 'Italy')
ORDER BY name;
```

</CodeExample>

**`NOT IN`** does the opposite. These are the films that are **not** comedies, dramas, thrillers or sci-fi:

<CodeExample output={`         title         |   genre
-----------------------+-----------
 Mountain of Echoes    | adventure
 Ninja Pandas II       | action
 Tea for Two Thousand  | romance
 The Great Bamboo Race | family
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT title, genre
FROM movies
WHERE genre NOT IN ('comedy', 'drama', 'thriller', 'sci-fi')
ORDER BY title;
```

</CodeExample>

## BETWEEN: a range of values

**`BETWEEN low AND high`** keeps values in a range. Both ends are **included**, so this finds films from 2018, 2019 and 2020:

<CodeExample output={`        title        | year
---------------------+------
 Code Red            | 2018
 The Bamboo Heist    | 2019
 Snow Leopard Summer | 2020
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT title, year
FROM movies
WHERE year BETWEEN 2018 AND 2020
ORDER BY year;
```

</CodeExample>

It means exactly the same as `year >= 2018 AND year <= 2020`. Put the smaller value first: `BETWEEN 2020 AND 2018` finds nothing.

## LIKE and ILIKE: matching patterns

Sometimes you only know **part** of a text value. **`LIKE`** matches a **pattern** with two special characters:

- **`%`** matches any number of characters (even none).
- **`_`** (underscore) matches exactly **one** character.

Products whose name **starts with** "Bamboo":

<CodeExample output={`       name        | price
-------------------+-------
 Bamboo Chopsticks |  4.20
 Bamboo Crisps     |  2.99
(2 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, price
FROM products
WHERE name LIKE 'Bamboo%'
ORDER BY name;
```

</CodeExample>

`'%Panda%'` means "Panda **anywhere** in the name", and `'_a%'` means "any one letter, then an a, then anything":

<CodeExample output={`     name
---------------
 Panda Cookies
 Plush Panda
(2 rows)

    name
------------
 Ravi Patel
 Sam Ortiz
 Zara Khan
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name
FROM products
WHERE name LIKE '%Panda%'
ORDER BY name;

SELECT name
FROM customers
WHERE name LIKE '_a%'
ORDER BY name;
```

</CodeExample>

`LIKE` cares about capitals, just like `=`. PostgreSQL's **`ILIKE`** ignores them, which is perfect for a search box:

<CodeExample output={`         title
-----------------------
 Ninja Pandas II
 Panda Express to Mars
(2 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT title
FROM movies
WHERE title ILIKE '%panda%'
ORDER BY title;
```

</CodeExample>

With `LIKE '%panda%'` this query would find nothing, because both titles spell "Panda" with a capital P. `NOT LIKE` and `NOT ILIKE` work too.

<Callout type="note" title="What about missing values?">

Two customers have no city stored at all. That "nothing" is a special value called `NULL`, and `WHERE city = NULL` doesn't find them! You'll learn why, and what to write instead, in [NULL: Missing Values](/learn/sql/null-values).

</Callout>

## Recap

- `WHERE condition` keeps only the rows where the condition is true. It goes after `FROM` and before `ORDER BY`.
- Compare with `=`, `<>`, `<`, `>`, `<=` and `>=`. Text and dates go in single quotes; text comparisons are exact.
- Combine conditions with `AND`, `OR` and `NOT`. `AND` comes before `OR`, so use brackets when you mix them.
- `IN (...)` matches any value in a list; `BETWEEN a AND b` matches a range, ends included.
- `LIKE` matches patterns: `%` is any number of characters, `_` is exactly one. `ILIKE` ignores capitals.

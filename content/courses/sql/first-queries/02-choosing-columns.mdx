`SELECT *` shows **everything**, which is great for a first look. But real questions usually need only a few columns: "What's the name and price of each product?" In this lesson you'll choose your columns, give them friendly names, calculate brand-new columns and get rid of duplicate rows.

## Picking the columns you want

Instead of `*`, write the column names you want, separated by **commas**:

<CodeExample output={`    name     | location
-------------+----------
 Engineering | London
 Design      | London
 Sales       | Madrid
 Support     | Tokyo
 Research    | Berlin
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, location FROM departments;
```

</CodeExample>

The columns come back in the order **you** list them, so you can rearrange them however you like:

<CodeExample output={` location |    name
----------+-------------
 London   | Engineering
 London   | Design
 Madrid   | Sales
 Tokyo    | Support
 Berlin   | Research
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT location, name FROM departments;
```

</CodeExample>

<Callout type="warning" title="Commas between, not after">

Put a comma **between** column names, but not after the last one. `SELECT name, location, FROM departments;` is a syntax error, because the database expects one more column after that last comma.

</Callout>

## Rows in a predictable order

Bigger tables print more rows, so from now on our examples add one more line at the end, `ORDER BY`, to **sort** the result. Read `ORDER BY title` as "sorted by title, A to Z". You'll learn everything about it in the next module; for now it just keeps the rows tidy and in the same order every time.

<CodeExample output={`         title          | year
------------------------+------
 Bug in the Machine     | 2025
 Code Red               | 2018
 Midnight in the Forest | 2015
 Mountain of Echoes     | 2016
 Ninja Pandas II        | 2022
 Panda Express to Mars  | 2023
 Snow Leopard Summer    | 2020
 Tea for Two Thousand   | 2017
 The Bamboo Heist       | 2019
 The Great Bamboo Race  | 2014
 The Last Dumpling      | 2021
 The Quiet Algorithm    | 2024
(12 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT title, year
FROM movies
ORDER BY title;
```

</CodeExample>

Once a query has three parts or more, we put **each part on its own line**. The database doesn't mind either way, but it's much easier for people to read.

## Renaming columns with AS

The column names in a table are often short or technical. **`AS`** gives a column a new name in the result, called an **alias**:

<CodeExample output={` department  |  city
-------------+--------
 Engineering | London
 Design      | London
 Sales       | Madrid
 Support     | Tokyo
 Research    | Berlin
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name AS department, location AS city
FROM departments;
```

</CodeExample>

An alias only changes the **result**. The table itself still has its columns called `name` and `location`.

## Single quotes and double quotes

SQL uses two kinds of quotes, and they mean very different things:

- **Single quotes** `'like this'` are for **text values** (also called **strings**): words and sentences that are data.
- **Double quotes** `"like this"` are for **names** of tables, columns and aliases.

You only need double quotes for a name with spaces, capital letters or symbols. Without them, PostgreSQL turns names into lower case:

<CodeExample output={`  greeting   | Department name | shoutyname
-------------+-----------------+------------
 Hello, SQL! | Engineering     | London
 Hello, SQL! | Design          | London
 Hello, SQL! | Sales           | Madrid
 Hello, SQL! | Support         | Tokyo
 Hello, SQL! | Research        | Berlin
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT 'Hello, SQL!' AS greeting,
  name AS "Department name",
  location AS ShoutyName
FROM departments;
```

</CodeExample>

- `'Hello, SQL!'` is a text value, so it appears in **every row**, exactly as written.
- `"Department name"` keeps its space and capital letter thanks to the double quotes.
- `ShoutyName` had no quotes, so it became `shoutyname`.

Mix them up and you get a confusing error. With double quotes, PostgreSQL looks for a **column** called `hello`:

<CodeExample output={`ERROR:  column "hello" does not exist
LINE 1: SELECT "hello";
               ^`}>

```sql
SELECT "hello";
```

</CodeExample>

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep names simple">

Aliases like `"Department name"` are fine for a final report, but while you work, simple lower-case names with underscores, like `department_name`, save you from typing quotes all the time.

</Callout>

## Calculating new columns

A column in the result doesn't have to come from the table. You can **calculate** it with an **expression**, such as a sum. You don't even need a table for that:

<CodeExample output={` answer | product | big
--------+---------+------
      5 |      40 | 1024
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT 2 + 3 AS answer, 10 * 4 AS product, 2 ^ 10 AS big;
```

</CodeExample>

The maths operators are `+`, `-`, `*` (multiply), `/` (divide) and `^` (power). Expressions get really useful with columns, because the database works them out **for every row**:

<CodeExample output={`         title          | year | age_in_2025 | seconds
------------------------+------+-------------+---------
 Bug in the Machine     | 2025 |           0 |    6420
 Code Red               | 2018 |           7 |    6540
 Midnight in the Forest | 2015 |          10 |    6720
 Mountain of Echoes     | 2016 |           9 |    7080
 Ninja Pandas II        | 2022 |           3 |    6120
 Panda Express to Mars  | 2023 |           2 |    7260
 Snow Leopard Summer    | 2020 |           5 |    5880
 Tea for Two Thousand   | 2017 |           8 |    5580
 The Bamboo Heist       | 2019 |           6 |    6240
 The Great Bamboo Race  | 2014 |          11 |    5280
 The Last Dumpling      | 2021 |           4 |    5700
 The Quiet Algorithm    | 2024 |           1 |    7860
(12 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT title, year,
  2025 - year AS age_in_2025,
  minutes * 60 AS seconds
FROM movies
ORDER BY title;
```

</CodeExample>

Always give a calculated column an alias. Without one, PostgreSQL makes up a name like `?column?`, which isn't very helpful.

<Callout type="warning" title="Dividing whole numbers">

When both numbers are whole numbers, `/` drops the remainder: `SELECT 7 / 2;` gives `3`, not `3.5`. Write `7 / 2.0` to get `3.5000000000000000`. You'll learn all about numbers in the Text, Numbers & Dates module.

</Callout>

## Removing duplicates with DISTINCT

Which genres are in the film catalogue? Selecting `genre` gives one value **per film**, so `comedy` would appear twice. Add **`DISTINCT`** right after `SELECT` to keep each different value only once:

<CodeExample output={`   genre
-----------
 action
 adventure
 comedy
 drama
 family
 romance
 sci-fi
 thriller
(8 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT DISTINCT genre
FROM movies
ORDER BY genre;
```

</CodeExample>

With **several columns**, `DISTINCT` removes rows where the **whole combination** is repeated. Each director makes films in two genres, so every director appears twice, but each director + genre pair appears only once:

<CodeExample output={`  director  |   genre
------------+-----------
 Ada Brooks | drama
 Ada Brooks | thriller
 Kenji Mori | action
 Kenji Mori | adventure
 Mei Lin    | comedy
 Mei Lin    | family
 Ravi Patel | sci-fi
 Ravi Patel | thriller
 Sam Ortiz  | romance
 Sam Ortiz  | sci-fi
(10 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT DISTINCT director, genre
FROM movies
ORDER BY director, genre;
```

</CodeExample>

Ada Brooks directed two dramas, but the pair `Ada Brooks | drama` shows up just once.

The shop wants to know how much money is sitting on its shelves. Write a query on `products` that shows four columns, in this order:

1. `name`
2. `price`
3. `stock` (how many are in the warehouse)
4. `stock_value`: the price multiplied by the stock

Show every product. You don't need to sort the rows.

<Hint>

Add `stock` to the list of columns, then one more column calculated with `price * stock`. Give that calculation the alias `stock_value` with `AS`.

```sql
  price * stock AS stock_value
FROM products;
```

## Recap

- `SELECT col1, col2 FROM table;` shows only the columns you list, in your order.
- `AS` gives a result column a new name (an **alias**).
- **Single quotes** are for text values; **double quotes** are for names with spaces or capitals.
- Expressions like `price * stock` calculate a new value for every row. Always give them an alias.
- `DISTINCT` removes duplicate rows; with several columns it looks at the whole combination.
- `ORDER BY` at the end sorts the result (more in the next module).

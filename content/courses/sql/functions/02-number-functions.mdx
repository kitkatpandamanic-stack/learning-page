Databases are brilliant calculators. Prices with tax, monthly salaries, how many boxes you need to ship your stock: PostgreSQL can work all of it out while it reads the rows, so you don't need a spreadsheet next to your query. 🧮

In this lesson you'll do maths in SQL, learn the one trap that catches almost everybody (dividing whole numbers), round results nicely, and convert values from one **type** to another.

## The arithmetic operators

SQL has the operators you know from school, plus one you might not:

| Operator | Meaning | Example | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `+` | add | `7 + 2` | `9` |
| `-` | subtract | `7 - 2` | `5` |
| `*` | multiply | `7 * 2` | `14` |
| `/` | divide | `7 / 2` | `3` (!) |
| `%` | remainder after dividing | `7 % 2` | `1` |

You can use them on columns, on numbers you type, or both. Brackets work like in maths: `(price + 1) * 2` adds first, then multiplies. Here's what each product's stock is worth, and what it would cost with 20% tax added:

<CodeExample output={`       name        | price | stock | stock_value | with_tax
-------------------+-------+-------+-------------+----------
 Tea Pot           | 32.00 |     6 |      192.00 |   38.400
 Bamboo Chopsticks |  4.20 |   200 |      840.00 |    5.040
 Rice Cooker       | 89.90 |     4 |      359.60 |  107.880
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  name,
  price,
  stock,
  price * stock AS stock_value,
  price * 1.2 AS with_tax
FROM products
WHERE category = 'kitchen'
ORDER BY id;
```

</CodeExample>

The calculations are done for **every row**, using that row's values. `price * 1.2` has three decimal places because `price` has two and `1.2` has one. You'll fix that with `round` in a moment.

## The integer division trap

Look at the table again: `7 / 2` gives `3`, not `3.5`! When **both** sides of `/` are whole numbers (**integers**), PostgreSQL does **integer division**: it divides and throws away everything after the decimal point. The `%` operator gives you the part that was thrown away, the **remainder**.

This bites when you calculate monthly salaries. `salary` is an integer column and `12` is an integer:

<CodeExample output={`     name     | salary | monthly_wrong |        monthly
--------------+--------+---------------+-----------------------
 Grace Hopper | 150000 |         12500 |    12500.000000000000
 Alan Turing  | 130000 |         10833 |    10833.333333333333
 Ada Lovelace |  98000 |          8166 | 8166.6666666666666667
 Linus Park   |  72000 |          6000 | 6000.0000000000000000
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  name,
  salary,
  salary / 12 AS monthly_wrong,
  salary / 12.0 AS monthly
FROM employees
ORDER BY id
LIMIT 4;
```

</CodeExample>

Alan Turing just lost 0.33 of his monthly pay! 😱 The fix is to make at least one side a decimal number: write `12.0` instead of `12`, and PostgreSQL does real division.

Integer division isn't always a mistake, though. Together with `%`, it's exactly what you want for "how many full boxes, and how many are left over":

<CodeExample output={`     name      | stock | full_boxes | left_over
---------------+-------+------------+-----------
 Bamboo Crisps |   120 |         10 |         0
 Panda Cookies |    85 |          7 |         1
 Rice Crackers |    60 |          5 |         0
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  name,
  stock,
  stock / 12 AS full_boxes,
  stock % 12 AS left_over
FROM products
WHERE category = 'snacks'
ORDER BY id;
```

</CodeExample>

## Rounding: round, ceil, floor

Long decimals are ugly in a report. PostgreSQL has a family of rounding functions:

- **`round(x, n)`** rounds to `n` decimal places (halves go away from zero: `2.5` becomes `3`). Without `n`, it rounds to a whole number.
- **`ceil(x)`** ("ceiling") always rounds **up** to the next whole number.
- **`floor(x)`** always rounds **down**.
- **`trunc(x, n)`** simply cuts off the extra digits, without rounding.
- **`abs(x)`** removes the minus sign.

<CodeExample output={` round_2 | round_0 | ceil | floor | trunc | abs
---------+---------+------+-------+-------+-----
    2.57 |       3 |    3 |     2 |   2.5 |   4
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  round(2.567, 2) AS round_2,
  round(2.5) AS round_0,
  ceil(2.1) AS ceil,
  floor(2.9) AS floor,
  trunc(2.567, 1) AS trunc,
  abs(-4) AS abs;
```

</CodeExample>

`ceil` is the one for "how many do I need?" questions: 25 guests at tables of 6 need `ceil(25 / 6.0)`, which is 5 tables, even though the last one isn't full. Now the monthly salaries, properly rounded to pennies:

<CodeExample output={`    name     | monthly
-------------+---------
 Don Norman  | 7916.67
 Susan Kare  | 5333.33
 Dieter Rams | 5083.33
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, round(salary / 12.0, 2) AS monthly
FROM employees
WHERE department_id = 2
ORDER BY monthly DESC;
```

</CodeExample>

Notice that `ORDER BY` can use the alias `monthly`: sorting happens after the `SELECT` list is calculated.

## Types and casting with ::

Every value in PostgreSQL has a **type**. The ones you'll meet most:

- **`integer`**: whole numbers like `42` (the `stock` and `salary` columns),
- **`numeric`**: exact decimal numbers like `6.50` (the `price` column is `numeric(8, 2)`: up to 8 digits, 2 of them after the point),
- **`text`**: strings like `'Kite'`,
- plus dates, times and true/false values (`boolean`), which you'll meet soon.

**Casting** converts a value to another type. PostgreSQL's short way is **`::`** followed by the type name. The SQL-standard way, `CAST(value AS type)`, does the same:

<CodeExample output={`      name       | price | whole_price | from_text | doubled | label
-----------------+-------+-------------+-----------+---------+--------
 The Bamboo Code | 12.50 |          13 |        43 |    5.00 | 7 left
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  name,
  price,
  price::integer AS whole_price,
  '42'::integer + 1 AS from_text,
  CAST('2.50' AS numeric) * 2 AS doubled,
  stock::text || ' left' AS label
FROM products
WHERE id = 7;
```

</CodeExample>

- Casting a decimal to `integer` rounds it: `12.50` becomes `13`.
- `'42'::integer` turns the **text** `'42'` into the **number** `42`, so you can add to it.
- `stock::text` turns a number into text. (`||` would do this for you anyway, but now you know how.)

Casting is also the second fix for integer division: `salary::numeric / 12` makes one side a `numeric`, so the division is exact.

If the value can't be converted, you get an error instead of a guess:

```text
SELECT 'abc'::integer;

ERROR:  invalid input syntax for type integer: "abc"
```

## numeric vs float: exact or fast

PostgreSQL has two kinds of decimal numbers:

- **`numeric`** stores decimals **exactly**, digit by digit. It's what you want for **money** and anything you'd add up and check.
- **`float8`** (also called `double precision`) and `real` are **floating-point** numbers, the kind most programming languages use. They're fast and good for measurements and science, but they store most decimals as a tiny bit off.

<CodeExample output={` numeric_sum | numeric_equal |      float_sum      | float_equal
-------------+---------------+---------------------+-------------
         0.3 | t             | 0.30000000000000004 | f
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  0.1 + 0.2 AS numeric_sum,
  0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3 AS numeric_equal,
  0.1::float8 + 0.2::float8 AS float_sum,
  0.1::float8 + 0.2::float8 = 0.3 AS float_equal;
```

</CodeExample>

(`t` and `f` are how PostgreSQL prints **true** and **false**.) A decimal you type, like `0.1`, is `numeric` by default, so the sums come out exact. The floats are off by a tiny amount, and `=` says they're not equal. Over millions of payments those tiny errors add up to real money.

<Callout type="warning" title="Money is numeric">

Store prices, balances and totals as `numeric` (with a scale, like `numeric(10, 2)`), never as `float8` or `real`. And round only at the end, when you show the result: rounding every step adds small errors, too.

</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="round needs numeric">

`round(x, 2)` with decimal places only works on `numeric`. With a `float8` you'll see `function round(double precision, integer) does not exist`. The fix is a cast: `round(x::numeric, 2)`.

</Callout>

## Recap

- `+ - * /` work on columns and numbers, row by row. **`%`** gives the remainder.
- **Integer division**: if both sides of `/` are integers, the decimals are dropped (`7 / 2` is `3`). Use `12.0` or a cast like `salary::numeric` for exact division.
- **`round(x, n)`** rounds, **`ceil`** rounds up ("how many do I need?"), **`floor`** rounds down, **`trunc`** cuts, **`abs`** drops the sign.
- **`value::type`** (or `CAST(value AS type)`) converts between types like `integer`, `numeric` and `text`; impossible conversions are errors.
- **`numeric`** is exact (use it for money); **`float8`** is fast but slightly inexact. `round(x, n)` needs a `numeric`.

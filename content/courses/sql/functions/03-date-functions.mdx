"How many orders came in last month?", "Who joined on a weekend?", "When should this parcel arrive?" Almost every real question about data has a **date** in it somewhere. PostgreSQL is very good with dates: it knows how long each month is, which years are leap years, and which weekday any date falls on. 📅

In this lesson you'll write dates, calculate with them, and turn them into exactly the text you want in a report.

## Date and time types

PostgreSQL has a few types for moments in time. These three cover most needs:

| Type | Holds | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `date` | a calendar day | `2025-03-01` |
| `timestamp` | a day **and** a time of day | `2025-03-01 14:30:00` |
| `interval` | a length of time | `2 days 03:00:00` |

In the sample database, `customers.joined_on` and `employees.hired_on` are dates, and `orders.ordered_at` is a timestamp. (Real apps often use **`timestamptz`**, a timestamp that knows about time zones. You'll meet it when you design your own tables in Level 1.)

To write a date or time yourself, put the type name in front of a string in **`YYYY-MM-DD`** order (year, month, day, the international standard), or cast a string with `::`:

<CodeExample output={`   a_date   |     a_timestamp     |   an_interval   | christmas
------------+---------------------+-----------------+------------
 2025-03-01 | 2025-03-01 14:30:00 | 2 days 03:00:00 | 2025-12-25
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  date '2025-03-01' AS a_date,
  timestamp '2025-03-01 14:30' AS a_timestamp,
  interval '2 days 3 hours' AS an_interval,
  '2025-12-25'::date AS christmas;
```

</CodeExample>

Always use `YYYY-MM-DD`: is `03/04/2025` the 3rd of April or the 4th of March? It depends on the country, so don't make PostgreSQL guess. Impossible dates are rejected:

```text
SELECT date '2025-02-30';

ERROR:  date/time field value out of range: "2025-02-30"
```

## Filtering by date

Dates compare just like numbers: earlier is smaller. In `WHERE`, PostgreSQL turns a string like `'2025-03-01'` into a date for you, because it can see that you compare it with a date column:

<CodeExample output={` id |     ordered_at      |  status
----+---------------------+-----------
 14 | 2025-03-02 06:58:00 | cancelled
 15 | 2025-03-06 10:15:00 | delivered
 16 | 2025-03-10 13:32:00 | delivered
 17 | 2025-03-14 16:49:00 | shipped
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT id, ordered_at, status
FROM orders
WHERE ordered_at >= '2025-03-01'
  AND ordered_at < '2025-03-15'
ORDER BY id;
```

</CodeExample>

<Callout type="tip" title="Ranges: from the start, up to (not including) the end">

For "all of March", write `ordered_at >= '2025-03-01' AND ordered_at < '2025-04-01'`. It's tempting to write `BETWEEN '2025-03-01' AND '2025-03-31'`, but a timestamp of `2025-03-31 18:00` is **later** than `2025-03-31` (which means midnight), so the last day's orders would quietly go missing. "From the start, up to the next start" is always right.

</Callout>

## Calculating with dates

You can do maths with dates and times, and PostgreSQL handles the calendar for you:

- **date + number** adds that many days, and **date - date** gives the number of days between them.
- **+ interval** adds any length of time: `interval '1 month'`, `interval '90 minutes'`, `interval '2 weeks'`.
- **timestamp - timestamp** gives an interval.

<CodeExample output={` plus_30_days | days_to_christmas |    plus_a_month     |        later        |   gap
--------------+-------------------+---------------------+---------------------+----------
 2025-03-31   |               299 | 2025-02-28 00:00:00 | 2025-03-01 16:00:00 | 10:58:00
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  date '2025-03-01' + 30 AS plus_30_days,
  date '2025-12-25' - date '2025-03-01' AS days_to_christmas,
  date '2025-01-31' + interval '1 month' AS plus_a_month,
  timestamp '2025-03-01 14:30' + interval '90 minutes' AS later,
  timestamp '2025-03-02 06:58' - timestamp '2025-03-01 20:00' AS gap;
```

</CodeExample>

Look at `plus_a_month`: January 31st plus a month is February **28th**, because February 2025 has no 31st. Also notice it's a timestamp now (adding an interval gives a timestamp). Cast it back with `::date` if you only want the day.

Here's a real use: when was each of the first orders due to arrive, if delivery takes 3 days?

<CodeExample output={` id |     ordered_at      | order_day  | arrives_on
----+---------------------+------------+------------
  1 | 2025-01-07 12:17:00 | 2025-01-07 | 2025-01-10
  2 | 2025-01-11 15:34:00 | 2025-01-11 | 2025-01-14
  3 | 2025-01-15 18:51:00 | 2025-01-15 | 2025-01-18
  4 | 2025-01-19 22:08:00 | 2025-01-19 | 2025-01-22
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  id,
  ordered_at,
  ordered_at::date AS order_day,
  ordered_at::date + 3 AS arrives_on
FROM orders
ORDER BY id
LIMIT 4;
```

</CodeExample>

Casting a timestamp to `::date` drops the time of day.

## Pulling out parts with extract

**`extract(part FROM value)`** takes one piece out of a date or timestamp, as a number. Useful parts are `year`, `month`, `day`, `hour`, `minute`, and **`dow`** (day of week: 0 is Sunday, 6 is Saturday):

<CodeExample output={` id |     ordered_at      | year | month | weekday | hour
----+---------------------+------+-------+---------+------
  1 | 2025-01-07 12:17:00 | 2025 |     1 |       2 |   12
  2 | 2025-01-11 15:34:00 | 2025 |     1 |       6 |   15
  3 | 2025-01-15 18:51:00 | 2025 |     1 |       3 |   18
  4 | 2025-01-19 22:08:00 | 2025 |     1 |       0 |   22
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  id,
  ordered_at,
  extract(year FROM ordered_at) AS year,
  extract(month FROM ordered_at) AS month,
  extract(dow FROM ordered_at) AS weekday,
  extract(hour FROM ordered_at) AS hour
FROM orders
ORDER BY id
LIMIT 4;
```

</CodeExample>

`extract` is great in `WHERE`. Which customers joined in the summer months?

<CodeExample output={`     name     | joined_on
--------------+------------
 Lucas Silva  | 2024-06-07
 Anna Ivanova | 2024-06-25
 Yuki Tanaka  | 2024-07-14
 Chloe Martin | 2024-08-02
 Noah Smith   | 2024-08-21
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, joined_on
FROM customers
WHERE extract(month FROM joined_on) IN (6, 7, 8)
ORDER BY joined_on;
```

</CodeExample>

## Rounding dates with date_trunc

**`date_trunc(unit, value)`** cuts a timestamp down to the start of its `'year'`, `'month'`, `'week'` (weeks start on Monday), `'day'` or `'hour'`. It's the date version of `floor`, and you'll use it all the time for monthly and weekly reports:

<CodeExample output={` id |     ordered_at      |        month        | week_start
----+---------------------+---------------------+------------
  5 | 2025-01-24 01:25:00 | 2025-01-01 00:00:00 | 2025-01-20
  6 | 2025-01-28 04:42:00 | 2025-01-01 00:00:00 | 2025-01-27
  7 | 2025-02-01 07:59:00 | 2025-02-01 00:00:00 | 2025-01-27
  8 | 2025-02-05 11:16:00 | 2025-02-01 00:00:00 | 2025-02-03
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  id,
  ordered_at,
  date_trunc('month', ordered_at) AS month,
  date_trunc('week', ordered_at)::date AS week_start
FROM orders
WHERE id IN (5, 6, 7, 8)
ORDER BY id;
```

</CodeExample>

Orders 5 and 6 were both placed in January, so they get the same `month` value, `2025-01-01 00:00:00`; orders 7 and 8 both get February. That makes orders from the same month easy to put together into one group, which is exactly what you'll do in the next module with `GROUP BY`.

## Formatting with to_char

**`to_char(value, pattern)`** turns a date or timestamp into text, in any format you like. The pattern is made of codes that get replaced:

| Code | Means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `YYYY` | year | `2025` |
| `MM` | month number | `03` |
| `Mon` / `Month` | month name | `Mar` / `March` |
| `DD` | day of the month | `07` |
| `Dy` / `Day` | weekday | `Tue` / `Tuesday` |
| `HH24:MI` | hours and minutes | `14:30` |
| `FM` | in front of a code: no padding | `FMDay`, `FMDD` |

<CodeExample output={` id | short_date  |       long_date       | time  |  month
----+-------------+-----------------------+-------+---------
  1 | 07 Jan 2025 | Tuesday, 7 January    | 12:17 | 2025-01
  2 | 11 Jan 2025 | Saturday, 11 January  | 15:34 | 2025-01
  3 | 15 Jan 2025 | Wednesday, 15 January | 18:51 | 2025-01
(3 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  id,
  to_char(ordered_at, 'DD Mon YYYY') AS short_date,
  to_char(ordered_at, 'FMDay, FMDD FMMonth') AS long_date,
  to_char(ordered_at, 'HH24:MI') AS time,
  to_char(ordered_at, 'YYYY-MM') AS month
FROM orders
ORDER BY id
LIMIT 3;
```

</CodeExample>

Without `FM`, PostgreSQL pads names with spaces so they all have the same width (`Tuesday` is padded to 9 characters, like `Wednesday`) and numbers with zeros (`07`). `FM` ("fill mode") turns that off.

<Callout type="warning" title="Format last, sort by the real date">

`to_char` gives **text**, and text sorts letter by letter: `'Apr'` comes before `'Jan'`. Do your filtering, grouping and sorting with real dates (or `date_trunc`), and use `to_char` only to make the final result pretty. The `'YYYY-MM'` format is the exception: it happens to sort correctly, which makes it popular in reports.

</Callout>

## How long ago: age

**`age(later, earlier)`** gives the time between two dates in years, months and days, the way people say it. Combine it with `extract(year FROM ...)` for whole years, for example years of service on the last day of 2025:

<CodeExample output={`     name     |  hired_on  |         service         | years
--------------+------------+-------------------------+-------
 Grace Hopper | 2015-03-01 | 10 years 9 mons 30 days |    10
 Marie Curie  | 2016-02-02 | 9 years 10 mons 29 days |     9
 Alan Turing  | 2016-06-15 | 9 years 6 mons 16 days  |     9
 Mary Kay     | 2016-10-03 | 9 years 2 mons 28 days  |     9
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT
  name,
  hired_on,
  age(date '2025-12-31', hired_on) AS service,
  extract(year FROM age(date '2025-12-31', hired_on)) AS years
FROM employees
ORDER BY hired_on
LIMIT 4;
```

</CodeExample>

<Callout type="note" title="What about today?">

**`current_date`** is today's date, **`now()`** is the current date and time, and `age(hired_on)` with only one date counts up to today:

```sql
SELECT name, age(hired_on) AS service
FROM employees;

SELECT id, ordered_at
FROM orders
WHERE ordered_at >= now() - interval '30 days';
```

You'll use them constantly in real apps ("orders in the last 30 days"). In these lessons we use fixed dates like `date '2025-12-31'` instead, so the results don't change every day, and the sample data stays in 2025 forever. 🐼

</Callout>

The shop promises delivery within 5 days. The manager wants a list of every order placed in **March 2025** with:

- **`id`**: the order's id,
- **`order_day`**: the day it was placed, as a date (no time),
- **`weekday`**: the weekday's full name, like `Sunday`, without extra spaces,
- **`deliver_by`**: the order day plus 5 days, as a date.

Sort by `id`. Make sure an order placed at `2025-03-31 23:30` would still count as March!

<Hint>

Filter with `ordered_at >= '2025-03-01' AND ordered_at < '2025-04-01'`. `ordered_at::date` drops the time, and adding `5` to a date adds five days. For the weekday, `to_char(ordered_at, 'FMDay')` (the `FM` removes the padding spaces).

```sql
SELECT
  id,
  ordered_at::date AS order_day,
  to_char(ordered_at, 'FMDay') AS weekday,
  ordered_at::date + 5 AS deliver_by
FROM orders
WHERE ordered_at >= '2025-03-01'
  AND ordered_at < '2025-04-01'
ORDER BY id;
```

</Solution>

## Recap

- **`date`** is a day, **`timestamp`** a day and time, **`interval`** a length of time. Write them as `date '2025-03-01'` or `'2025-03-01'::date`, always in `YYYY-MM-DD` order.
- Compare dates in `WHERE` like numbers. For a whole month, use `>= start AND < next start`.
- `date + 5` adds days, `date - date` gives days, `+ interval '1 month'` adds any length of time, and `::date` drops the time.
- **`extract(part FROM x)`** pulls out the year, month, day, hour or weekday (`dow`) as a number.
- **`date_trunc('month', x)`** rounds down to the start of the month (or year, week, day…), ready for monthly reports.
- **`to_char(x, 'DD Mon YYYY')`** formats dates as text: use it last, for display.
- **`age(later, earlier)`** gives years, months and days between two dates. `now()` and `current_date` exist, but these lessons use fixed dates.

In Part 1 you found films. Now the club wants **numbers**: how long the films really are, how old they are, and which genres and directors are the club's favourites. This is where calculations, functions and grouping come in. 🧮

Everything here works on **one table**, `movies`, and every answer is a single query.

## Runtimes people can read

"131 minutes" is hard to picture; "2h 11m" is easy. Integer **division** `/` gives the whole hours, and `%` (the **remainder**) gives the minutes left over. `concat()` glues the pieces into one piece of text:

<CodeExample output={`         title          | minutes | hours | extra_minutes | runtime
------------------------+---------+-------+---------------+---------
 The Quiet Algorithm    |     131 |     2 |            11 | 2h 11m
 Panda Express to Mars  |     121 |     2 |             1 | 2h 1m
 Mountain of Echoes     |     118 |     1 |            58 | 1h 58m
 Midnight in the Forest |     112 |     1 |            52 | 1h 52m
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT title,
  minutes,
  minutes / 60 AS hours,
  minutes % 60 AS extra_minutes,
  concat(minutes / 60, 'h ', minutes % 60, 'm') AS runtime
FROM movies
ORDER BY minutes DESC
LIMIT 4;
```

</CodeExample>

`131 / 60` is `2` (not 2.18…) because both numbers are **integers**, so PostgreSQL drops the fraction. Here that's exactly what we want. When you *do* want the fraction, make one side a decimal:

<CodeExample output={` wrong_hours | hours
-------------+-------
         1.0 |   1.7
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT round(minutes / 60, 1) AS wrong_hours,
  round(minutes / 60.0, 1) AS hours
FROM movies
WHERE title = 'The Bamboo Heist';
```

</CodeExample>

*The Bamboo Heist* runs 104 minutes. `104 / 60` is already `1` before `round()` sees it, so rounding can't bring the `.7` back. Dividing by `60.0` keeps the fraction: 1.7 hours.

## Labels and ages

The club's posters show each film as *Title (Year)*. The `||` operator joins text, and numbers like `year` are turned into text automatically. For a film's age, the club counts from **2025**, the year of this season's programme:

<CodeExample output={`             film              |  director  | years_old
-------------------------------+------------+-----------
 The Great Bamboo Race (2014)  | MEI LIN    |        11
 Midnight in the Forest (2015) | ADA BROOKS |        10
 Mountain of Echoes (2016)     | KENJI MORI |         9
 Tea for Two Thousand (2017)   | SAM ORTIZ  |         8
(4 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT title || ' (' || year || ')' AS film,
  upper(director) AS director,
  2025 - year AS years_old
FROM movies
ORDER BY year
LIMIT 4;
```

</CodeExample>

<Callout type="note" title="Why a fixed year?">

`extract(year FROM current_date)` would give *today's* year, so the answer would change every January. Reports that must be repeatable, like this one, use a fixed date. When the club starts its next season, it changes one number.

</Callout>

## Planning the meeting

The next meeting is on **14 November 2025**. `to_char()` formats a date for humans, and adding a number to a `date` adds that many **days**. The `FM` prefix removes the padding spaces that `Day` and `Month` would otherwise add:

<CodeExample output={`         meeting          | next_meeting
--------------------------+--------------
 Friday, 14 November 2025 | 2025-11-21
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT to_char(date '2025-11-14', 'FMDay, FMDD FMMonth YYYY') AS meeting,
  date '2025-11-14' + 7 AS next_meeting;
```

</CodeExample>

## The catalogue in one row

**Aggregate functions** squash many rows into one. A single query gives the club its headline numbers:

<CodeExample output={` films | rated | avg_rating | total_minutes | total_hours
-------+-------+------------+---------------+-------------
    12 |    12 |       7.21 |          1278 |        21.3
(1 row)`}>

```sql
SELECT count(*) AS films,
  count(rating) AS rated,
  round(avg(rating), 2) AS avg_rating,
  sum(minutes) AS total_minutes,
  round(sum(minutes) / 60.0, 1) AS total_hours
FROM movies;
```

</CodeExample>

Watching the whole catalogue back to back would take over **21 hours**. 🍿

`count(*)` counts **rows**, while `count(rating)` counts rows where `rating` is **not NULL**. Both are 12, which tells us no film is missing its rating. That's a quick data-quality check worth doing on any column you're about to average, because `avg()` silently skips NULLs.

## Genres and decades with GROUP BY

`GROUP BY` makes one group per value and runs the aggregates **per group**. Which genres do we have most of, and how good are they?

<CodeExample output={`   genre   | films | avg_rating | longest
-----------+-------+------------+---------
 comedy    |     2 |        7.5 |     104
 drama     |     2 |        7.9 |     112
 sci-fi    |     2 |        7.7 |     131
 thriller  |     2 |        6.8 |     109
 action    |     1 |        5.9 |     102
 adventure |     1 |        7.9 |     118
 family    |     1 |        6.5 |      88
 romance   |     1 |        6.7 |      93
(8 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT genre,
  count(*) AS films,
  round(avg(rating), 1) AS avg_rating,
  max(minutes) AS longest
FROM movies
GROUP BY genre
ORDER BY films DESC, genre;
```

</CodeExample>

You can group by a **calculated value** too. Integer division turns a year into its decade: `2019 / 10 * 10` is `201 * 10`, which is `2010`:

<CodeExample output={` decade | films | avg_rating
--------+-------+------------
   2010 |     6 |       7.23
   2020 |     6 |       7.18
(2 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT year / 10 * 10 AS decade,
  count(*) AS films,
  round(avg(rating), 2) AS avg_rating
FROM movies
GROUP BY decade
ORDER BY decade;
```

</CodeExample>

A perfect split: six films from the 2010s and six from the 2020s, with almost the same average rating.

## Favourite directors with HAVING

`WHERE` filters **rows** before grouping; `HAVING` filters **groups** after it. Which directors have at least three films in the catalogue?

<CodeExample output={`  director  | films
------------+-------
 Ada Brooks |     3
 Mei Lin    |     3
(2 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT director, count(*) AS films
FROM movies
GROUP BY director
HAVING count(*) >= 3
ORDER BY director;
```

</CodeExample>

<Callout type="tip" title="WHERE or HAVING?">

Ask yourself: *can I check this on a single film?* "Rated 7 or more" can, so it belongs in `WHERE`. "Has at least three films" needs the whole group, so it belongs in `HAVING`. You can use both in one query.

</Callout>

The club wants to run a **director spotlight** night, featuring a director with plenty of films. Write one query that shows, for each director with **at least 3 films**:

- `director`,
- `films`: how many films they have in the catalogue,
- `avg_rating`: their average rating, **rounded to 2 decimal places**,
- `total_runtime`: all their films' minutes added up, written like `5h 17m`.

Sort by `avg_rating`, **highest first**, then by `director`. Today you should get two rows: Ada Brooks (`7.60`, `5h 17m`) and Mei Lin (`7.17`, `4h 47m`).

<Hint>

Group by `director` and keep big groups with `HAVING count(*) >= 3`. For the runtime, do the hours-and-minutes trick on `sum(minutes)`: `concat(sum(minutes) / 60, 'h ', sum(minutes) % 60, 'm')`. You can sort by an alias: `ORDER BY avg_rating DESC, director`.

</Hint>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT director,
  count(*) AS films,
  round(avg(rating), 2) AS avg_rating,
  concat(sum(minutes) / 60, 'h ', sum(minutes) % 60, 'm') AS total_runtime
FROM movies
GROUP BY director
HAVING count(*) >= 3
ORDER BY avg_rating DESC, director;
```

</Solution>

## Recap

- Integer `/` drops the fraction and `%` gives the remainder: together they turn minutes into **hours and minutes**. Divide by `60.0` when you need decimals.
- `||` and `concat()` build labels; `upper()`, `to_char()` and date arithmetic format and plan.
- Use **fixed dates** in reports that must give the same answer every time.
- `count(*)` vs `count(column)` is a quick check for missing values.
- `GROUP BY` works on columns **and** on calculated values like the decade.
- `WHERE` filters rows, `HAVING` filters groups.

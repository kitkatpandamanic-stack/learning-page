`GROUP BY` is great at answering "per department" questions, but it pays a price: it **squashes** each group into one row. Many real questions need the detail **and** the summary side by side:

- How does each engineer's salary compare with their team's average?
- Which are the three best-selling products **in every category**?
- What is each order's position in the "biggest orders" league table?

**Window functions** answer all of these. They calculate across a set of rows (the **window**) but, unlike `GROUP BY`, they keep every row in the result. They're one of the most useful tools in SQL, and the reason many analysts fall in love with it. 🪟

## GROUP BY squashes rows

Here's the average salary per department, the Level 0 way:

<CodeExample output={` department  | avg_salary
-------------+------------
 Design      |      73333
 Engineering |      91667
 Research    |      92000
 Sales       |      59250
 Support     |      49167
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT d.name AS department, round(avg(e.salary)) AS avg_salary
FROM employees AS e
JOIN departments AS d ON d.id = e.department_id
GROUP BY d.name
ORDER BY d.name;
```

</CodeExample>

Five departments, five rows. The individual employees are gone: you can't put `e.name` in the `SELECT` list, because a department row has many names. To show each person **next to** their department's average you'd need a subquery and a join. Window functions make it one line.

## OVER: an aggregate that keeps the rows

Add **`OVER ()`** after an aggregate function and it becomes a window function. The empty brackets mean "the window is every row of the result":

<CodeExample output={`       name        |   job_title   | salary | team_avg | team_size
-------------------+---------------+--------+----------+-----------
 Alan Turing       | CTO           | 130000 |    91667 |         6
 Ada Lovelace      | Lead Engineer |  98000 |    91667 |         6
 Linus Park        | Engineer      |  72000 |    91667 |         6
 Margaret Hamilton | Engineer      |  76000 |    91667 |         6
 Ken Thompson      | Engineer      |  69000 |    91667 |         6
 Barbara Liskov    | Architect     | 105000 |    91667 |         6
(6 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, job_title, salary,
  round(avg(salary) OVER ()) AS team_avg,
  count(*) OVER () AS team_size
FROM employees
WHERE department_id = 1
ORDER BY id;
```

</CodeExample>

All six engineers are still there, and every row also carries the average and the head count of the whole result. Notice that the window only sees the **six** rows left after `WHERE`: window functions run **after** the rows have been filtered (and grouped), which will matter in a minute.

## PARTITION BY: one window per group

**`PARTITION BY`** splits the rows into groups, and the function is calculated separately inside each one. It's like `GROUP BY`, except the rows stay:

<CodeExample output={`    name     | department_id | salary | dept_avg |  diff
-------------+---------------+--------+----------+--------
 Don Norman  |             2 |  95000 |    73333 |  21667
 Susan Kare  |             2 |  64000 |    73333 |  -9333
 Dieter Rams |             2 |  61000 |    73333 | -12333
 Mary Kay    |             3 |  90000 |    59250 |  30750
 Elena Gomez |             3 |  54000 |    59250 |  -5250
 Carlos Ruiz |             3 |  52000 |    59250 |  -7250
 Paul Allen  |             3 |  41000 |    59250 | -18250
(7 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT name, department_id, salary,
  round(avg(salary) OVER (PARTITION BY department_id)) AS dept_avg,
  salary - round(avg(salary) OVER (PARTITION BY department_id))
    AS diff
FROM employees
WHERE department_id IN (2, 3)
ORDER BY department_id, salary DESC;
```

</CodeExample>

Design's three people see Design's average (73,333) and Sales' four see Sales' (59,250). Now "who earns more than their team average?" is a simple `diff > 0`. You can partition by several columns too: `PARTITION BY country, city`.

Any aggregate works as a window function: `sum`, `count`, `avg`, `min`, `max`, even `string_agg`.

## Numbering rows: ROW_NUMBER, RANK and DENSE_RANK

Put an **`ORDER BY` inside `OVER`** and you can number rows in that order. PostgreSQL has three numbering functions, and they only differ when there are **ties**. The shop has some orders with exactly the same total, so let's rank the first quarter's orders:

<CodeExample output={` id | total  | row_number | rank | dense_rank
----+--------+------------+------+------------
  2 | 200.17 |          1 |    1 |          1
 17 | 200.17 |          2 |    1 |          1
  5 | 149.73 |          3 |    3 |          2
 20 | 149.73 |          4 |    3 |          2
  7 | 124.10 |          5 |    5 |          3
  1 | 102.00 |          6 |    6 |          4
 16 | 102.00 |          7 |    6 |          4
 11 |  78.50 |          8 |    8 |          5
(8 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH order_totals AS (
  SELECT o.id, o.ordered_at, sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS total
  FROM orders AS o
  JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
  GROUP BY o.id
)
SELECT id, total,
  row_number() OVER (ORDER BY total DESC, id) AS row_number,
  rank() OVER (ORDER BY total DESC) AS rank,
  dense_rank() OVER (ORDER BY total DESC) AS dense_rank
FROM order_totals
WHERE ordered_at < '2025-04-01'
ORDER BY total DESC, id
LIMIT 8;
```

</CodeExample>

- **`row_number()`** gives 1, 2, 3, 4… with no repeats, even for ties. Orders 2 and 17 both total 200.17, so which one is first? Only the tie-breaker `id` decides. Without it PostgreSQL may pick either, and the answer can change between runs. **Always give `row_number()` a unique tie-breaker.**
- **`rank()`** gives ties the same number, then **skips**: 1, 1, 3, 3, 5. Like a race where two runners share gold and the next one gets bronze.
- **`dense_rank()`** gives ties the same number **without gaps**: 1, 1, 2, 2, 3.

<Callout type="warning" title="ORDER BY inside OVER doesn't sort the result">

The `ORDER BY` inside `OVER (...)` only says how to number the rows. The rows of the result can come out in any order. If you care about the order of the output (you nearly always do), add a normal `ORDER BY` at the end of the query, as above.

</Callout>

## Ranking inside each group

Combine both ideas: `PARTITION BY` starts the numbering again for every group, and `ORDER BY` decides the order inside it. Here's everyone in Engineering and Research, ranked by salary within their department:

<CodeExample output={` department_id |       name        | salary | salary_rank
---------------+-------------------+--------+-------------
             1 | Alan Turing       | 130000 |           1
             1 | Barbara Liskov    | 105000 |           2
             1 | Ada Lovelace      |  98000 |           3
             1 | Margaret Hamilton |  76000 |           4
             1 | Linus Park        |  72000 |           5
             1 | Ken Thompson      |  69000 |           6
             5 | Marie Curie       | 112000 |           1
             5 | Rosalind Franklin |  83000 |           2
             5 | Max Planck        |  81000 |           3
(9 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT department_id, name, salary,
  rank() OVER (
    PARTITION BY department_id
    ORDER BY salary DESC
  ) AS salary_rank
FROM employees
WHERE department_id IN (1, 5)
ORDER BY department_id, salary_rank;
```

</CodeExample>

The ranking restarts at 1 for department 5. This is the heart of the most famous window-function task of all: **top N per group**.

## Top N per group

"The two best-paid people in each department." Easy: filter on `salary_rank <= 2`... except it isn't:

<CodeExample output={`ERROR:  window functions are not allowed in WHERE
LINE 3: WHERE rank() OVER (
              ^`}>

```sql
SELECT department_id, name, salary
FROM employees
WHERE rank() OVER (
  PARTITION BY department_id ORDER BY salary DESC
) <= 2;
```

</CodeExample>

PostgreSQL runs the parts of a query in a fixed **logical order**, and window functions come late, after `WHERE` has already decided which rows exist:

```text
FROM / JOIN  →  WHERE  →  GROUP BY  →  HAVING
  →  window functions  →  SELECT list  →  DISTINCT  →  ORDER BY  →  LIMIT
```

So you can't filter on a window function in the same query's `WHERE` (or `HAVING`). The fix is to calculate the rank in one step and filter in the next, with a CTE or a subquery:

<CodeExample output={` department  | position |       name        | salary
-------------+----------+-------------------+--------
 Design      |        1 | Don Norman        |  95000
 Design      |        2 | Susan Kare        |  64000
 Engineering |        1 | Alan Turing       | 130000
 Engineering |        2 | Barbara Liskov    | 105000
 Research    |        1 | Marie Curie       | 112000
 Research    |        2 | Rosalind Franklin |  83000
 Sales       |        1 | Mary Kay          |  90000
 Sales       |        2 | Elena Gomez       |  54000
 Support     |        1 | Hana Sato         |  70000
 Support     |        2 | Aiko Suzuki       |  39500
(10 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH ranked AS (
  SELECT d.name AS department, e.name, e.salary,
    row_number() OVER (
      PARTITION BY e.department_id
      ORDER BY e.salary DESC, e.id
    ) AS position
  FROM employees AS e
  JOIN departments AS d ON d.id = e.department_id
)
SELECT department, position, name, salary
FROM ranked
WHERE position <= 2
ORDER BY department, position;
```

</CodeExample>

Which numbering function you pick changes the meaning of "top 2":

- **`row_number()`**: exactly two rows per group, ties broken by your tie-breaker. Right for "show two products in each tile on the home page".
- **`rank()`**: everyone tied with second place is included too, so a group can return three or more rows. Right for "award a prize to the top two scores".
- **`dense_rank()`**: the top two **distinct values**, with everyone who has them.

<Callout type="tip" title="Top 1 per group: DISTINCT ON">

For the special case of **one** row per group, PostgreSQL has a neat shortcut: `DISTINCT ON (expression)` keeps the first row of each group according to the `ORDER BY`, which must start with the same expression.

```sql
SELECT DISTINCT ON (department_id) department_id, name, salary
FROM employees
WHERE department_id IS NOT NULL
ORDER BY department_id, salary DESC, id;
```

It's PostgreSQL-only, though. Some other databases (Snowflake, BigQuery, DuckDB) instead offer a `QUALIFY` clause that filters on window functions directly; PostgreSQL doesn't have one, so the CTE pattern above is the portable answer.

</Callout>

## Windows on top of GROUP BY

Window functions run **after** `GROUP BY`, so they can work on the grouped rows. That lets you rank groups, or compare each group with the grand total, in one query. Here's the revenue of each category (cancelled orders don't count), its rank, and its share of all revenue:

<CodeExample output={` category | revenue | revenue_rank | pct
----------+---------+--------------+------
 kitchen  | 1160.10 |            1 | 47.4
 books    |  830.41 |            2 | 34.0
 toys     |  287.40 |            3 | 11.8
 snacks   |   86.91 |            4 |  3.6
 tea      |   81.00 |            5 |  3.3
(5 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT p.category,
  sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS revenue,
  rank() OVER (ORDER BY sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) DESC)
    AS revenue_rank,
  round(100 * sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price)
    / sum(sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price)) OVER (), 1) AS pct
FROM order_items AS oi
JOIN orders AS o ON o.id = oi.order_id
JOIN products AS p ON p.id = oi.product_id
WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
GROUP BY p.category
ORDER BY revenue DESC;
```

</CodeExample>

`sum(sum(...)) OVER ()` looks odd at first, so read it from the inside out: the inner `sum` is the normal `GROUP BY` aggregate (one revenue per category), and the outer `sum(...) OVER ()` adds those category revenues up across the whole result. The shares add up to 100% (give or take rounding).

Try it yourself: switch `rank()` to `dense_rank()` or `row_number()`, rank orders per **customer** with `PARTITION BY customer_id`, or show the top 3 instead of the top 2.

<TryIt language="sql" code={`WITH order_totals AS (
  SELECT o.id, o.customer_id,
    sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS total
  FROM orders AS o
  JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
  GROUP BY o.id
),
ranked AS (
  SELECT customer_id, id AS order_id, total,
    rank() OVER (
      PARTITION BY customer_id
      ORDER BY total DESC
    ) AS biggest
  FROM order_totals
)
SELECT *
FROM ranked
WHERE biggest <= 2 AND customer_id <= 4
ORDER BY customer_id, biggest, order_id;`} />

## Recap

- A **window function** calculates across a set of rows (the window) but keeps every row, unlike `GROUP BY`.
- `OVER ()` makes the window the whole result; **`PARTITION BY`** makes one window per group.
- `ORDER BY` inside `OVER` numbers rows: **`row_number()`** (1, 2, 3, 4), **`rank()`** (1, 1, 3, 4) and **`dense_rank()`** (1, 1, 2, 3). Give `row_number()` a unique tie-breaker.
- Window functions run after `WHERE`, `GROUP BY` and `HAVING`, so to filter on them (**top N per group**) compute them in a CTE and filter outside it.
- They also work on grouped rows: `rank() OVER (ORDER BY sum(x) DESC)` and `sum(sum(x)) OVER ()` rank groups and compare them with the total.
- `DISTINCT ON` is PostgreSQL's shortcut for top **1** per group.

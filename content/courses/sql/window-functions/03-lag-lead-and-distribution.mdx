"Did revenue grow since last month?" "How many days pass between a customer's orders?" "Is this film in the top quarter of the catalogue?" All of these compare a row with **other specific rows**: the one before, the one after, the best one in the group.

This lesson finishes the window-function toolbox with **offset functions** (`lag`, `lead`), **value functions** (`first_value`, `last_value`, `nth_value`) and **distribution functions** (`ntile`, `percent_rank`, `cume_dist`). They all use the same `OVER (PARTITION BY ... ORDER BY ...)` you already know. 🔍

## LAG: look at the previous row

**`lag(expression)`** returns the value of `expression` from the **previous row** in the window's order. The first row has no previous row, so it gets `NULL`. That's all you need for **period-over-period** reports:

<CodeExample output={`  month  | revenue | prev_revenue | change  | change_pct
---------+---------+--------------+---------+------------
 2025-01 |  518.30 |         NULL |    NULL |       NULL
 2025-02 |  331.69 |       518.30 | -186.61 |      -36.0
 2025-03 |  525.80 |       331.69 |  194.11 |       58.5
 2025-04 |  331.69 |       525.80 | -194.11 |      -36.9
 2025-05 |  467.80 |       331.69 |  136.11 |       41.0
 2025-06 |  270.54 |       467.80 | -197.26 |      -42.2
(6 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH monthly AS (
  SELECT to_char(o.ordered_at, 'YYYY-MM') AS month,
    sum(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price) AS revenue
  FROM orders AS o
  JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
  WHERE o.status <> 'cancelled'
  GROUP BY month
),
compared AS (
  SELECT month, revenue,
    lag(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS prev_revenue
  FROM monthly
)
SELECT month, revenue, prev_revenue,
  revenue - prev_revenue AS change,
  round(100 * (revenue - prev_revenue)
    / nullif(prev_revenue, 0), 1) AS change_pct
FROM compared
ORDER BY month;
```

</CodeExample>

A few details worth copying into your own reports:

- The second CTE gives `lag(...)` a name, so the final `SELECT` can use `prev_revenue` three times instead of repeating the window.
- **`nullif(prev_revenue, 0)`** turns a zero into `NULL`, so a month after a month with no revenue gives `NULL` instead of a "division by zero" error.
- The first month has no previous month, so its change is `NULL`. That's honest: "growth since nothing" isn't 0%.

`lag` takes two optional arguments: **`lag(x, n, default)`** looks `n` rows back (default 1) and returns `default` instead of `NULL` when there's no such row. On monthly data, `lag(revenue, 12)` gives **year-over-year** comparisons.

## LEAD: look at the next row

**`lead()`** is the mirror image: the value from the **next** row. Combined with `PARTITION BY`, the pair of them describe a customer's rhythm: how long since their last order, and when (or whether) they came back:

<CodeExample output={`    name    | order_id |    day     | days_since_prev | next_order
------------+----------+------------+-----------------+------------
 Mei Lin    |       17 | 2025-03-14 |            NULL | 2025-05-24
 Mei Lin    |       34 | 2025-05-24 |              71 | NULL
 Ravi Patel |        3 | 2025-01-15 |            NULL | 2025-03-27
 Ravi Patel |       20 | 2025-03-27 |              71 | 2025-06-05
 Ravi Patel |       37 | 2025-06-05 |              70 | NULL
 Sam Ortiz  |        5 | 2025-01-24 |            NULL | 2025-04-04
 Sam Ortiz  |       22 | 2025-04-04 |              70 | 2025-06-13
 Sam Ortiz  |       39 | 2025-06-13 |              70 | NULL
(8 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT c.name, o.id AS order_id, o.ordered_at::date AS day,
  o.ordered_at::date - lag(o.ordered_at::date) OVER w
    AS days_since_prev,
  lead(o.ordered_at::date) OVER w AS next_order
FROM orders AS o
JOIN customers AS c ON c.id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.customer_id IN (1, 2, 5)
WINDOW w AS (PARTITION BY o.customer_id ORDER BY o.ordered_at)
ORDER BY c.name, o.ordered_at;
```

</CodeExample>

Subtracting one `date` from another gives a whole number of days. A `NULL` in `next_order` means "no later order (yet)". In a real shop, "last order more than 90 days ago and no next order" is a classic definition of a customer who might be **churning**, and a good list for a "we miss you" email.

<Callout type="warning" title="The previous row isn't always the previous month">

`lag` looks at the previous **row**, not the previous month. If a month has no orders at all, it has no row, and `lag` happily compares May with March. When gaps are possible, build a **calendar** with `generate_series` first and `LEFT JOIN` the data onto it, so every month has a row (with 0 if nothing happened).

</Callout>

Here's that fix for Mei Lin, who only ordered in March and May:

<CodeExample output={`  month  | orders | prev_month
---------+--------+------------
 2025-01 |      0 |       NULL
 2025-02 |      0 |          0
 2025-03 |      1 |          0
 2025-04 |      0 |          1
 2025-05 |      1 |          0
 2025-06 |      0 |          1
(6 rows)`}>

```sql
WITH months AS (
  SELECT to_char(m, 'YYYY-MM') AS month
  FROM generate_series(date '2025-01-01', date '2025-06-01',
    interval '1 month') AS m
),
meis_orders AS (
  SELECT to_char(ordered_at, 'YYYY-MM') AS month, count(*) AS orders
  FROM orders
  WHERE customer_id = 1
  GROUP BY month
)
SELECT m.month, coalesce(mo.orders, 0) AS orders,
  lag(coalesce(mo.orders, 0)) OVER (ORDER BY m.month) AS prev_month
FROM months AS m
LEFT JOIN meis_orders AS mo ON mo.month = m.month
ORDER BY m.month;
```

</CodeExample>

Without the calendar, the query would have two rows, and May's "previous month" would be March.

## FIRST_VALUE and LAST_VALUE

**`first_value(x)`** and **`last_value(x)`** return `x` from the first or last row of the **frame**. Compare everyone with their department's top earner, and try to find the lowest earner too:

<CodeExample output={` department_id |    name     | salary | top_earner | lowest_oops | lowest_earner
---------------+-------------+--------+------------+-------------+---------------
             3 | Mary Kay    |  90000 | Mary Kay   | Mary Kay    | Paul Allen
             3 | Elena Gomez |  54000 | Mary Kay   | Elena Gomez | Paul Allen
             3 | Carlos Ruiz |  52000 | Mary Kay   | Carlos Ruiz | Paul Allen
             3 | Paul Allen  |  41000 | Mary Kay   | Paul Allen  | Paul Allen
             4 | Hana Sato   |  70000 | Hana Sato  | Hana Sato   | Taro Yamada
             4 | Aiko Suzuki |  39500 | Hana Sato  | Aiko Suzuki | Taro Yamada
             4 | Taro Yamada |  38000 | Hana Sato  | Taro Yamada | Taro Yamada
(7 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT department_id, name, salary,
  first_value(name) OVER by_salary AS top_earner,
  last_value(name) OVER by_salary AS lowest_oops,
  last_value(name) OVER (
    PARTITION BY department_id
    ORDER BY salary DESC
    ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND UNBOUNDED FOLLOWING
  ) AS lowest_earner
FROM employees
WHERE department_id IN (3, 4)
WINDOW by_salary AS (PARTITION BY department_id ORDER BY salary DESC)
ORDER BY department_id, salary DESC;
```

</CodeExample>

`first_value` works, but `lowest_oops` just repeats each person's own name. Remember the default frame from the [previous lesson](/learn/sql/running-totals-and-frames): with `ORDER BY` it ends at the **current row**, so the "last row of the frame" is the current row (or its last peer). To see the whole partition, spell out `ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND UNBOUNDED FOLLOWING`, as `lowest_earner` does. (Or flip the order and use `first_value`, which is often simpler.)

**`nth_value(x, n)`** returns the value from the n-th row of the frame: `nth_value(name, 2)` is the runner-up. It has the same frame catch as `last_value`.

## NTILE, PERCENT_RANK and CUME_DIST

The last family places each row **within the distribution** of all rows:

- **`ntile(n)`** splits the ordered rows into `n` buckets as equal in size as possible and returns the bucket number: `ntile(4)` gives quartiles, `ntile(10)` deciles, `ntile(100)` percentiles.
- **`percent_rank()`** is `(rank - 1) / (rows - 1)`: 0 for the first row, 1 for the last.
- **`cume_dist()`** is "the fraction of rows that come before or tie with this one", between 0 and 1.

<CodeExample output={`         title          | rating | quartile | pct_rank | cume_dist
------------------------+--------+----------+----------+-----------
 The Quiet Algorithm    |    8.4 |        1 |     1.00 |      1.00
 Midnight in the Forest |    8.1 |        1 |     0.91 |      0.92
 Mountain of Echoes     |    7.9 |        1 |     0.82 |      0.83
 The Bamboo Heist       |    7.8 |        2 |     0.73 |      0.75
 Snow Leopard Summer    |    7.6 |        2 |     0.64 |      0.67
 The Last Dumpling      |    7.2 |        2 |     0.55 |      0.58
 Bug in the Machine     |    7.1 |        3 |     0.45 |      0.50
 Panda Express to Mars  |    6.9 |        3 |     0.36 |      0.42
 Tea for Two Thousand   |    6.7 |        3 |     0.27 |      0.33
 The Great Bamboo Race  |    6.5 |        4 |     0.18 |      0.25
 Code Red               |    6.4 |        4 |     0.09 |      0.17
 Ninja Pandas II        |    5.9 |        4 |     0.00 |      0.08
(12 rows)`}>

```sql
SELECT title, rating,
  ntile(4) OVER (ORDER BY rating DESC) AS quartile,
  round(percent_rank() OVER (ORDER BY rating)::numeric, 2)
    AS pct_rank,
  round(cume_dist() OVER (ORDER BY rating)::numeric, 2)
    AS cume_dist
FROM movies
ORDER BY rating DESC;
```

</CodeExample>

Twelve films in four buckets gives three per quartile: the top three are "top 25%". `percent_rank` and `cume_dist` return `double precision`, so they're cast to `numeric` before `round(x, 2)` (rounding to decimal places only works on `numeric`). Read `cume_dist = 0.75` for The Bamboo Heist as "75% of the films are rated the same or lower".

<Callout type="tip" title="ntile is about row counts, not values">

`ntile(4)` makes buckets with the same **number of rows**, even if that splits equal values: two films with the same rating can land in different quartiles. If you need value bands ("the cheapest 25% of the price range"), or a proper median, use `percentile_cont(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY price)`, an ordered-set aggregate that interpolates values. Pick deliberately; they answer different questions.

</Callout>

Try it: change `ntile(4)` to `ntile(3)`, partition the films by `genre`, or use `lead` to show the next film each director made.

The film club wants to see how each director's career went. For every film show:

- `director`, `year`, `title` and `rating`,
- `years_since_prev`: years since the **same director's** previous film,
- `rating_change`: this film's rating minus the previous film's rating,
- `NULL` in both for a director's first film,
- sorted by director, then year.

<Hint>

Use `lag(year)` and `lag(rating)` over `PARTITION BY director ORDER BY year`, and subtract: `year - lag(year) OVER w AS years_since_prev`. A `WINDOW w AS (...)` clause saves typing the window twice. Subtracting from `NULL` gives `NULL`, so first films need no special handling.

</Hint>

<Solution>

```sql
SELECT director, year, title, rating,
  year - lag(year) OVER w AS years_since_prev,
  rating - lag(rating) OVER w AS rating_change
FROM movies
WINDOW w AS (PARTITION BY director ORDER BY year, id)
ORDER BY director, year;
```

</Solution>

## Recap

- **`lag(x)`** / **`lead(x)`** read `x` from the previous / next row of the window's order; `lag(x, n, default)` looks further and replaces the missing `NULL`.
- Period over period: `revenue - lag(revenue)`, and divide by `nullif(prev, 0)` for percentages. Fill gaps with a `generate_series` calendar first, because `lag` counts rows, not months.
- **`first_value`**, **`last_value`** and **`nth_value`** pick values from the **frame**; for `last_value` and `nth_value`, widen it with `ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND UNBOUNDED FOLLOWING`.
- **`ntile(n)`** splits rows into n equal-sized buckets; **`percent_rank()`** and **`cume_dist()`** give each row's relative position from 0 to 1.
- With ranking from the [first lesson](/learn/sql/window-functions-and-ranking) and frames from the second, you now have the whole window-function toolbox. Next up: advanced queries with `CASE`, recursion and JSON.

Time to be a data analyst for real. 🐧 In this mini-project you'll explore the **Palmer Penguins** dataset: measurements of real penguins collected by Dr Kristen Gorman and the Palmer Station Long Term Ecological Research programme in Antarctica, between 2007 and 2009. It's a favourite with data scientists for practising, and it's free to use (CC0 licence).

The full dataset has 344 penguins of three species: **Adelie**, **Gentoo** and **Chinstrap**. We'll use a slice of 22 of them, typed in as CSV text, including the **missing values** that are in the real data.

## The analysis workflow

Professional analyses follow roughly the same steps, and so will we:

1. **Ask questions.** What do you want to find out?
2. **Load and inspect** the data: size, types, gaps.
3. **Clean** it, and note every decision you make.
4. **Analyse**: filter, group and calculate.
5. **Visualise** the answers with charts.
6. **Conclude**, honestly, including what the data *can't* tell you.

Our questions:

- How many penguins of each species do we have, and where do they live?
- Which species is the heaviest?
- Are males heavier than females?
- Do the species have differently shaped bills?
- Do penguins with longer flippers weigh more?

Each code example below repeats the data so that it runs on its own; feel free to scroll past the CSV lines.

## Clean

Our decisions, which we'd write down in a real report:

- A penguin with no measurements can't help with any of our questions, so we **drop** it.
- A penguin with a missing sex still has useful measurements, so we **keep** it and label its sex `"unknown"` instead of guessing.
- Body mass is easier to read in kilograms, so we add a `body_mass_kg` column.

    count=("body_mass_kg", "count"),
    mean_kg=("body_mass_kg", "mean"),
    max_kg=("body_mass_kg", "max"),

- **Gentoo** penguins are clearly the heaviest, at about 5 kg on average. Adelie and Chinstrap penguins are similar, around 3.5 to 3.7 kg.

- Grouping by two columns gives a Series with a two-level index. **`unstack()`** turns the inner level (`sex`) into columns, giving a neat table. We left out the `"unknown"` penguins so they don't form their own column.

Gentoo bills are long and thin, Adelie bills short and deep. Now look at the second table: the two **longest** bills belong to Chinstraps, yet their ratio is lower, because Chinstrap bills are deep as well as long. Length alone can't tell a Gentoo from a Chinstrap, but length and depth together can. That's how biologists tell species apart, and it's the classic "can a computer tell the species from the measurements?" exercise in machine learning.

## Conclusions

A good analysis ends with short, honest answers:

- We measured **21 penguins** after dropping 1 with no measurements; 1 more has an unknown sex.
- **Gentoo** penguins are the heaviest (about 5.0 kg on average), Adelie the lightest (about 3.6 kg).
- **Males are heavier** than females in all three species.
- Gentoo bills are **long and thin**, Adelie bills **short and deep**.
- Flipper length and body mass are strongly related (a correlation of 0.87), partly because of the species difference.

<Callout type="warning" title="Small samples, careful claims">

21 penguins is a tiny sample. With 6 or 7 birds per species, one unusual penguin can move an average a lot, and we can't say whether a difference is real or luck without statistics (that's what tests like the t-test are for). Also, **correlation is not causation**: long flippers don't *cause* weight. Always say how much data an answer is based on. Try the same questions on the full dataset: `pip install palmerpenguins` or download `penguins.csv`, and use `pd.read_csv("penguins.csv")`.

</Callout>

def load_penguins(text):
    df = pd.read_csv(io.StringIO(text))
    df = df.dropna(subset=["body_mass_g"])
    df["sex"] = df["sex"].fillna("unknown")
    return df

def species_counts(df):
    return df["species"].value_counts().to_dict()

def mean_mass(df):
    means = df.groupby("species")["body_mass_g"].mean()
    return means.round(1).to_dict()

def heaviest_species(df):
    means = df.groupby("species")["body_mass_g"].mean()
    return means.idxmax()

def species_on(df, island):
    on_island = df[df["island"] == island]
    return sorted(on_island["species"].unique())

## Recap

- An analysis follows a workflow: **questions → load and inspect → clean → analyse → visualise → conclude**.
- Inspect with `shape`, `head()` and `isna().sum()`; decide per column whether to **drop** or **fill** gaps, and write the decision down.
- `value_counts()` and `pd.crosstab()` count categories; `groupby().agg()` builds summary tables; grouping by two columns plus `unstack()` compares subgroups.
- New columns like a ratio can reveal things no single column shows.
- Bar charts compare groups; scatter plots show how two measurements relate, and `corr()` measures it.
- Be honest about sample size, and remember that correlation is not causation.
- Wrap cleaning and questions in **functions** so the analysis is reusable and testable.

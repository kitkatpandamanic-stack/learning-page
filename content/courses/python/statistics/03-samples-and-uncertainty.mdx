You asked 40 customers how much they'd spend, and the average was £32. Does that mean the average of **all** your customers is £32? Probably not exactly. Could it be £25? £40? A number without an idea of how wrong it might be is only half an answer.

This lesson is about that second half: **uncertainty**. Instead of heavy maths, you'll let Python **simulate** what could have happened, thousands of times, and see how much the answer wobbles. Then you'll use the same idea to answer the most common question in business data: *"is this difference real, or just luck?"* 🎲

## Populations and samples

The **population** is everything you want to know about: all your customers, every order, every visitor this year. You rarely see all of it. What you have is a **sample**: the 40 customers who answered, the visitors of one week.

A number calculated from a sample, like its mean, is an **estimate** of the population's number. Take a different sample and you get a slightly different estimate. That wobble is called **sampling variability**, and it's what the rest of this lesson measures.

<Callout type="warning" title="Randomness can be measured, bias can't">

Everything in this lesson assumes a **random** sample: every customer had the same chance of being picked. If you only asked customers who use the app, or people who chose to answer a survey, your sample is **biased**, and no formula can tell you by how much. More biased data just makes you more confident about the wrong answer. Think about *how* the data was collected before you compute anything.

</Callout>

## Random numbers you can repeat

Simulations need random numbers, and NumPy's **random generator** is the tool. You create one with **`np.random.default_rng(seed)`**: the **seed** makes the "random" numbers the same on every run, so your results can be checked and repeated, like `random.seed()` in [Modules and Imports](/learn/python/modules-and-imports).

<CodeExample output={`[1 5 4 3 3 6 1 5 2 1]
[0.976 0.761 0.786]
[169.9 163.2 177. ]
['A' 'B' 'B' 'B' 'A' 'B']
[1 5 4 3 3 6 1 5 2 1]`}>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
print(rng.integers(1, 7, size=10))        # dice: 1 to 6
print(rng.random(3).round(3))             # between 0 and 1
print(rng.normal(170, 8, size=3).round(1))  # around 170
print(rng.choice(["A", "B"], size=6))     # pick from a list

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)           # same seed again...
print(rng.integers(1, 7, size=10))        # ...same dice
```

</CodeExample>

- **`rng.integers(low, high, size=...)`** gives whole numbers from `low` up to, but not including, `high`.
- **`rng.normal(mean, std, size=...)`** gives numbers that cluster around `mean` in a bell shape.
- **`rng.choice(values, size=...)`** picks from a list or array. By default it picks **with replacement**: the same item can come up twice, like rolling a die again. Remember this one, it's the star of the bootstrap below.

Create **one** generator at the start and pass it around (or pass the seed), rather than creating new ones in a loop. You'll still see the older `np.random.seed(42)` style in tutorials; the generator is the modern way.

## The law of large numbers

Flip a fair coin 10 times and you might get 8 heads. Flip it 100,000 times and you'll get very close to 50%. That's the **law of large numbers**: the average of a sample gets closer to the population's average as the sample grows.

<CodeExample output={`     10 flips: 0.700 heads
    100 flips: 0.540 heads
   1000 flips: 0.501 heads
  10000 flips: 0.494 heads
 100000 flips: 0.502 heads`}>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
for n in [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000, 100_000]:
    flips = rng.integers(0, 2, size=n)    # 1 = heads
    print(f"{n:>7} flips: {flips.mean():.3f} heads")
```

</CodeExample>

The mean of an array of 0s and 1s is the **proportion** of 1s, a trick you'll use all the time with "did they buy: yes/no" data. Here's how the running proportion settles down as the flips add up. The x axis is on a **log scale** (`set_xscale("log")`), so 10, 100 and 1,000 flips are equally spaced:

<TryIt language="python" code={`import numpy as np

`np.cumsum` gives running totals (1, 1, 2, 3...), and dividing by 1, 2, 3... turns them into running proportions. Early on the runs swing wildly; after a few thousand flips they all hug 0.5. **Small samples are noisy.** Remember this the next time someone shows you a 5-star rating from 3 reviews.

## How much do sample means wobble?

Let's play god for a moment. We'll make up a **population** of 100,000 order values, so we know the true average, then take many samples from it and see how far their means land from the truth:

<CodeExample output={`true mean £32.44, std £21.41
n =  25: means spread 4.28, formula 4.28
n = 100: means spread 2.12, formula 2.14
n = 400: means spread 1.07, formula 1.07`}>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
population = rng.lognormal(3.3, 0.6, size=100_000)   # £, skewed
sigma = population.std()
print(f"true mean £{population.mean():.2f}, std £{sigma:.2f}")

for n in [25, 100, 400]:
    # 2,000 samples of n orders: one sample per row
    samples = rng.choice(population, size=(2_000, n))
    means = samples.mean(axis=1)          # the mean of each row
    print(f"n = {n:>3}: means spread {means.std():.2f},"
          f" formula {sigma / np.sqrt(n):.2f}")
```

</CodeExample>

`size=(2_000, n)` makes a table with 2,000 rows, one sample per row, and **`mean(axis=1)`** averages **along each row**, giving 2,000 sample means at once, with no Python loop. (`axis=0` would average down each column instead.)

The spread of the sample means has a name: the **standard error (SE)**. It answers *"how far is a sample mean typically from the true mean?"* And the simulation agrees with a famous formula:

**SE = std / √n**

In real life you don't know the population's std, so you use the sample's: `sample.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(sample))`. The square root is the important part: **4 times more data only halves the uncertainty**. Going from 100 to 400 orders took the wobble from about £2 to about £1.

Plot the sample means and two more things appear. Try `n = 2`:

<TryIt language="python" code={`import numpy as np

The sample means get **narrower** as n grows (that's the standard error shrinking), and they form a **bell shape** even though single orders are skewed. That second fact is the **central limit theorem**, and it's why so many statistical formulas assume a bell curve: they're usually about *means*, and means of decent-sized samples behave like that.

## Confidence intervals with the bootstrap

In real life you don't have a population to sample from 2,000 times. You have **one** sample. The **bootstrap** is a brilliantly simple trick: treat your sample as a stand-in for the population, and **resample from it with replacement**.

1. From your n values, draw n values **with replacement** (some values appear twice, some not at all).
2. Compute the statistic you care about, like the mean.
3. Repeat thousands of times.
4. The middle 95% of those results is a **95% confidence interval**.

<CodeExample output={`sample mean £31.51
bootstrap 95% CI: £26.25 to £37.53
mean ± 1.96 SE:   £25.75 to £37.27
median £28.36, 95% CI £23.11 to £33.21`}>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
sample = rng.lognormal(3.3, 0.6, size=40).round(2)  # 40 orders
print(f"sample mean £{sample.mean():.2f}")

boot = rng.choice(sample, size=(10_000, len(sample)))
boot_means = boot.mean(axis=1)
low, high = np.percentile(boot_means, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"bootstrap 95% CI: £{low:.2f} to £{high:.2f}")

se = sample.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(sample))
print(f"mean ± 1.96 SE:   £{sample.mean() - 1.96 * se:.2f}"
      f" to £{sample.mean() + 1.96 * se:.2f}")

boot_medians = np.median(boot, axis=1)
low, high = np.percentile(boot_medians, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"median £{np.median(sample):.2f},"
      f" 95% CI £{low:.2f} to £{high:.2f}")
```

</CodeExample>

The bootstrap and the classic formula (mean ± 1.96 standard errors, where 1.96 comes from the bell curve) give similar answers. The bootstrap's big advantage: it works for **any** statistic, like the median above, a percentile or a ratio, for which there's no simple formula.

So how do you say it? *"Customers spend about £32 on average (95% CI: £26 to £38)."* A **95% confidence interval** comes from a method that, if you repeated the whole study many times, would capture the true value in about 95% of them. A wide interval is honest information: it says you need more data before betting on the exact number.

## Is the difference real? Hypothesis testing

The shop tried a new product page. 50 random visitors saw the old page (**A**) and 50 saw the new one (**B**). The average basket on B was higher. Is B better, or did B just happen to get a few big spenders?

Statisticians answer with a kind of proof by contradiction:

1. Assume the boring explanation, called the **null hypothesis**: *the page makes no difference.*
2. Ask: **if** that were true, how often would we see a difference **at least as big** as ours, just by luck?
3. If the answer is "hardly ever", the boring explanation looks unlikely, and we say the difference is **statistically significant**.

The **permutation test** does step 2 by simulation. If the page makes no difference, the labels A and B are meaningless, so we can **shuffle** them: pool all 100 baskets, deal them randomly into two groups of 50, and measure the difference. Repeat 10,000 times:

<CodeExample output={`A £29.64, B £33.89, difference £4.25
p-value: 0.0152`}>

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
a = rng.normal(30, 10, size=50).round(2)   # old page, £ baskets
b = rng.normal(35, 10, size=50).round(2)   # new page
observed = b.mean() - a.mean()
print(f"A £{a.mean():.2f}, B £{b.mean():.2f},"
      f" difference £{observed:.2f}")

pooled = np.concatenate([a, b])
n_perm = 10_000
# Each row: the 100 baskets in a random order
shuffled = rng.permuted(np.tile(pooled, (n_perm, 1)), axis=1)
diffs = (shuffled[:, len(a):].mean(axis=1)
         - shuffled[:, :len(a)].mean(axis=1))

p_value = np.mean(np.abs(diffs) >= abs(observed))
print(f"p-value: {p_value:.4f}")
```

</CodeExample>

- **`np.tile(pooled, (n_perm, 1))`** repeats the pooled array as 10,000 rows, and **`rng.permuted(..., axis=1)`** shuffles each row on its own. The first 50 columns become "fake A", the rest "fake B".
- We compare **absolute** differences (`np.abs`) because we'd also be interested if B were much *worse*. That's called a **two-sided** test, and it's the sensible default.
- The **p-value** is the fraction of shuffles that produced a difference at least as big as the real one.

Only about 1.5% of the shuffles came out as extreme as the real £4.25. If the page made no difference, we'd rarely see a gap this big, so with p < 0.05 we call the difference **statistically significant**.

Here's what those 10,000 "no real difference" worlds look like, with the real difference as a line:

(`~extreme` flips a mask: `True` becomes `False` and back.) Most shuffles give differences near £0, as they should when labels mean nothing. Our real difference sits out in the tail.

## What a p-value is, and isn't

The **p-value** is: *the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme **if the null hypothesis were true**.* A small p-value means "luck alone would rarely do this". By convention, **p < 0.05** is called significant, but 0.05 is a tradition, not a law of nature.

It's one of the most misunderstood numbers in science. A p-value is **not**:

- the probability that the null hypothesis is true (p = 0.03 does **not** mean "a 3% chance the pages are equal"),
- the probability that B is better,
- a measure of how **big** or how **important** the difference is.

And p > 0.05 doesn't prove there's **no** difference. It means this data can't tell: "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence".

## The same tests with scipy.stats

You now understand what tests do. In practice, **SciPy** (`scipy.stats`) has the standard ones ready-made, and they're fast because they use formulas instead of simulation:

<CodeExample output={`t-test p-value: 0.0152
chi-squared p-value: 0.0577
binomial p-value: 0.0569`}>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
a = rng.normal(30, 10, size=50).round(2)
b = rng.normal(35, 10, size=50).round(2)

# Two groups of numbers: Welch's t-test
result = stats.ttest_ind(b, a, equal_var=False)
print(f"t-test p-value: {result.pvalue:.4f}")

# Bought / didn't buy, for two variants: chi-squared test
#         bought  didn't
table = [[120, 880],      # A: 12.0% of 1,000
         [150, 850]]      # B: 15.0% of 1,000
chi2 = stats.chi2_contingency(table)
print(f"chi-squared p-value: {chi2.pvalue:.4f}")

# Is a coin fair? 60 heads in 100 flips
print(f"binomial p-value: {stats.binomtest(60, 100, 0.5).pvalue:.4f}")
```

</CodeExample>

- **`stats.ttest_ind(b, a, equal_var=False)`** compares the means of two groups of numbers. `equal_var=False` (Welch's version) doesn't assume both groups are equally spread, which is the safer default. Its p-value matches our permutation test's almost exactly.
- **`stats.chi2_contingency(table)`** compares **proportions**: a table of counts with one row per group. Conversion rates are exactly this kind of data. Notice that 12% vs 15% with 1,000 visitors each is *not quite* significant: differences between small percentages need lots of visitors.
- **`stats.binomtest(successes, trials, p)`** checks one proportion against an expected value. 60 heads in 100 is a bit suspicious, but not enough to call the coin unfair.

The permutation test and the bootstrap are worth knowing anyway: they work for any statistic and make you understand what the formulas are doing.

## Big samples, tiny effects

"Significant" means "probably not luck". It does **not** mean "big" or "important". With enough data, even a meaningless difference becomes significant:

<CodeExample output={`difference 0.18, p = 5.2e-09
effect size d = 0.02`}>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
a = rng.normal(50.0, 10, size=200_000)
b = rng.normal(50.2, 10, size=200_000)   # 0.2 better: tiny

p = stats.ttest_ind(b, a, equal_var=False).pvalue
pooled_std = np.sqrt((a.var(ddof=1) + b.var(ddof=1)) / 2)
d = (b.mean() - a.mean()) / pooled_std
print(f"difference {b.mean() - a.mean():.2f}, p = {p:.1e}")
print(f"effect size d = {d:.2f}")
```

</CodeExample>

The p-value is microscopic, but the difference is 0.2 on values around 50 that vary by about 10. That's why you should always report the **effect size** alongside the p-value: the difference itself with its confidence interval, or a standardised version like **Cohen's d** (difference ÷ standard deviation; roughly 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large). The question for a business is never just "is it real?" but **"is it real and big enough to matter?"**

## Common mistakes

These mistakes are so common that they have names:

- **Peeking.** Checking the p-value every day and stopping the test the first time it dips below 0.05. Random results wander, and if you look often enough they'll cross 0.05 by chance. Decide the **sample size before** you start, and analyse once at the end.
- **Many comparisons.** Test 20 things that have no real effect, and on average one of them will be "significant" at 0.05. Look:

<CodeExample output={`experiments with a 'significant' metric: 65%
theory: 1 - 0.95**20 = 64%`}>

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
# 1,000 experiments, each comparing 20 metrics, NO real effects
a = rng.normal(size=(1_000, 20, 50))
b = rng.normal(size=(1_000, 20, 50))
p = stats.ttest_ind(a, b, axis=2).pvalue     # shape (1000, 20)

lucky = (p < 0.05).any(axis=1).mean()
print(f"experiments with a 'significant' metric: {lucky:.0%}")
print(f"theory: 1 - 0.95**20 = {1 - 0.95 ** 20:.0%}")
```

</CodeExample>

- **p-hacking.** Trying different subgroups, date ranges or metrics until something is significant, then reporting only that. Every extra try is another lottery ticket.

The fixes: choose **one main metric** and the sample size up front, write the plan down, and if you do test many things, make the threshold stricter. The simplest rule, the **Bonferroni correction**, divides 0.05 by the number of tests (20 tests: use 0.0025).

<Callout type="tip" title="How pros report a result">

Say what you measured, the effect with its interval, and the p-value: *"The new page increased the average basket by £4.25 (95% CI: £0.84 to £7.66, p = 0.015, 50 visitors per page)."* Then a sentence on whether that's big enough to matter. Never just "it was significant".

</Callout>

```python
import numpy as np

def standard_error(values):
    values = np.asarray(values)
    return values.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(values))

def bootstrap_ci(values, stat=np.mean, n_boot=5_000,
                 level=0.95, seed=0):
    values = np.asarray(values)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    resamples = rng.choice(values, size=(n_boot, len(values)))
    results = stat(resamples, axis=1)
    tail = (1 - level) / 2 * 100
    low, high = np.percentile(results, [tail, 100 - tail])
    return float(low), float(high)

def permutation_p_value(a, b, n_perm=5_000, seed=0):
    a, b = np.asarray(a), np.asarray(b)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    observed = b.mean() - a.mean()
    pooled = np.concatenate([a, b])
    shuffled = rng.permuted(np.tile(pooled, (n_perm, 1)), axis=1)
    diffs = (shuffled[:, len(a):].mean(axis=1)
             - shuffled[:, :len(a)].mean(axis=1))
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(diffs) >= abs(observed)))

baskets = [31.5, 22.0, 48.2, 27.9, 35.1, 19.4, 41.0, 29.8,
           25.5, 38.7, 33.3, 24.6, 52.0, 28.4, 30.9, 36.2]
old = [30.1, 27.5, 33.8, 25.0, 29.4, 31.7, 26.8, 28.9]
new = [34.2, 36.9, 31.0, 38.4, 33.5, 35.8, 32.7, 37.1]

print(standard_error(baskets))
print(bootstrap_ci(baskets))
print(bootstrap_ci(baskets, stat=np.median, level=0.9))
print(permutation_p_value(old, new))
```

## Recap

- You usually see a **sample**, not the **population**; estimates wobble from sample to sample. Randomness can be measured, **bias** can't.
- **`np.random.default_rng(seed)`** makes repeatable random numbers: `integers`, `normal`, `choice` (with replacement by default), `permuted`.
- **Law of large numbers**: bigger samples land closer to the truth. Small samples are noisy.
- The **standard error** = std / √n measures how much a sample mean wobbles; 4× the data halves it.
- **Bootstrap**: resample your data with replacement thousands of times; the middle 95% of the results is a **95% confidence interval**, for any statistic.
- **Hypothesis test**: assume no difference (the **null hypothesis**), and ask how often luck alone gives a result this extreme. A **permutation test** answers by shuffling the labels.
- The **p-value** is not the chance the null is true, nor the size of the effect. Report the **effect size** and its interval too.
- `scipy.stats`: `ttest_ind(..., equal_var=False)` for means, `chi2_contingency` for proportions, `binomtest` for one proportion.
- Avoid **peeking**, **many comparisons** and **p-hacking**: fix the metric and sample size in advance.
